<div class="note-content" markdown="0">

<p>Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((M,g)\)</span>{% endraw %} be a smooth Riemannian manifold, with Levi–Civita connection {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\nabla\)</span>{% endraw %}. For an immersed submanifold, the tangent and normal parts of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\nabla\)</span>{% endraw %} give the induced connection and second fundamental form. See the <a href="{% post_url 2022-05-11-connections-lie-brackets-and-curvature %}">connection notes</a> for the notation.</p>

<div id="application-qualitative-geometric-results" data-note-section="title">

</div>
<p>Curvature bounds constrain the global behaviour of geodesics. Positive curvature can produce shortening variations, while nonpositive curvature prevents a nontrivial Jacobi field starting at zero from returning to zero. We apply these two mechanisms to fundamental groups, global exponential coordinates, and diameter bounds.</p>

<div id="thm:synge-even" class="theorem" data-note-number="1" data-note-kind="Theorem">
<p><strong>Theorem 1 (Synge).</strong> A connected, compact, oriented Riemannian manifold without boundary, of even dimension and strictly positive sectional curvature, is simply connected.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Suppose a nontrivial free homotopy class of loops exists. We first justify the shortest-loop argument. Compactness gives a uniform positive radius on which short geodesics joining nearby points are unique and depend smoothly on their endpoints. Indeed, apply the inverse function theorem to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((x,v)\mapsto(x,\exp_xv)\)</span>{% endraw %} near the compact zero section. Sufficiently short loops lie in contractible coordinate neighborhoods, so lengths in our class have positive infimum {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}. Take loops whose lengths decrease to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}, with constant-speed parametrizations on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([0,1]\)</span>{% endraw %}. Their uniform Lipschitz bound and compactness give a uniformly convergent subsequence. Nearby loops are freely homotopic by short geodesic connectors. Thus the limit is in the same class; lower semicontinuity of length makes it a minimizer. Replacing short subarcs by their unique minimizing geodesics preserves the class, so equality forces the minimizer to be locally geodesic, also across the periodic joining point. We obtain a smooth nonconstant closed unit-speed geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta:[0,\ell]\to M\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>Parallel transport around {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta\)</span>{% endraw %} preserves the metric and orientation and fixes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta'(0)=\eta'(\ell)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Its restriction to the normal space is therefore {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(P\in\mathrm{SO}(2d-1)\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\dim M=2d\)</span>{% endraw %}. An odd-dimensional orthogonal matrix with determinant {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1\)</span>{% endraw %} has eigenvalue {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1\)</span>{% endraw %}: nonreal eigenvalues pair with their conjugates, while the product of the remaining odd number of real eigenvalues {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pm1\)</span>{% endraw %} is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1\)</span>{% endraw %}. Parallel transport of a fixed unit vector gives a smooth periodic field {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y\perp\eta'\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|Y|=1\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y'=0\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>The variation {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c(s,t)=\exp_{\eta(t)}(sY(t))\)</span>{% endraw %} consists of closed loops in this free homotopy class. For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E(s)=\frac12\int_0^\ell|\partial_tc|^2\,dt\)</span>{% endraw %}, endpoint terms cancel by periodicity. Our curvature convention gives</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[E'(0)=0,\qquad E''(0)=\int_0^\ell\left(|Y'|^2-\langle R(\eta',Y)\eta',Y\rangle\right)\,dt=-\int_0^\ell K(\eta',Y)\,dt&lt;0.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E(s)&lt;E(0)=\ell/2\)</span>{% endraw %} for small nonzero {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s\)</span>{% endraw %}. Cauchy–Schwarz gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(L(c_s)^2\le2\ell E(s)&lt;\ell^2\)</span>{% endraw %}, a contradiction. There is no nontrivial free homotopy class, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi_1(M)=0\)</span>{% endraw %}. In dimension zero, connectedness makes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} a point. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<div id="thm:cartan-hadamard" class="theorem" data-note-number="2" data-note-kind="Theorem">
<p><strong>Theorem 2</strong> (Cartan-Hadamard).  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a complete, simply connected differentiable manifold with nonpositive sectional curvature, i.e. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K(p,\sigma) \leq 0\)</span>{% endraw %} for all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p \in M\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma \subset T_p M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is diffeomorphic to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb R^n\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n = \dim M\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p : T_p M \to M\)</span>{% endraw %} is a diffeomorphism.</p>

</div>
<p>The proof first excludes conjugate points, then equips {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T_pM\)</span>{% endraw %} with the pullback metric. Completeness makes the exponential map a covering local isometry, and simple connectivity makes this connected covering a diffeomorphism. An arbitrary immersion from a complete manifold need not be a covering map.</p>

<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> All manifolds here are connected and without boundary. Write {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T=\gamma'\)</span>{% endraw %}. A nonzero Jacobi field with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(J(0)=0\)</span>{% endraw %} has {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(J'(0)\ne0\)</span>{% endraw %} by uniqueness of its equation. Our curvature convention gives</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[(|J|^2)''=2|J'|^2-2\langle R(T,J)T,J\rangle\ge0.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Moreover {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|J(t)|^2=t^2|J'(0)|^2+O(t^3)\)</span>{% endraw %}, so its derivative is positive for small positive {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>{% endraw %}. Convexity keeps it positive thereafter. Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(J(t)\ne0\)</span>{% endraw %} for every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. The Jacobi differential formula makes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %} nonsingular away from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\)</span>{% endraw %}; its differential at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\)</span>{% endraw %} is the identity.</p>
<p>Completeness of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} defines {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %} on all of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T_pM\)</span>{% endraw %}. The pullback {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h=(\exp_p)^*g\)</span>{% endraw %} is a positive definite smooth metric. Gauss's lemma says that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r(v)=|v|_{g_p}\)</span>{% endraw %} satisfies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|dr|_h=1\)</span>{% endraw %} away from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Integrating along curves, including across {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\)</span>{% endraw %} by continuity, gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|r(v)-r(w)|\le d_h(v,w)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Hence an {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h\)</span>{% endraw %}-Cauchy sequence lies in a bounded Euclidean ball and has a Euclidean-convergent subsequence. A smooth positive definite metric induces the Euclidean topology locally, so this subsequence converges also for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_h\)</span>{% endraw %}. The Cauchy property then makes the full sequence converge. Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((T_pM,h)\)</span>{% endraw %} is complete.</p>
<p>We supply the covering argument. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f:(X,h)\to(Y,g)\)</span>{% endraw %} be a local isometry with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %} complete and both manifolds connected. A finite-length piecewise smooth path in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y\)</span>{% endraw %} starting at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f(x)\)</span>{% endraw %} lifts uniquely from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x\)</span>{% endraw %}: local inverses give the lift until a maximal time; near a finite endpoint its remaining length tends to zero, so the lift is Cauchy and converges in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %}. A local inverse at its limit extends the lift. Any two points in connected {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y\)</span>{% endraw %} can be joined by such a path, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} is surjective.</p>
<p>Choose a normal ball {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B(y,\varepsilon)\)</span>{% endraw %}. For every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x\in f^{-1}(y)\)</span>{% endraw %}, lifting radial segments gives the smooth branch</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[s_x(z)=\exp_x\bigl((df_x)^{-1}\exp_y^{-1}z\bigr),\qquad f\circ s_x=\mathrm{id}.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Geodesic completeness of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %} defines this throughout the ball. The images of distinct branches are disjoint: if they met, lifting the reversed radial path uniquely would identify their initial points over {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(y\)</span>{% endraw %}. Reversed radial lifting also shows that the branches exhaust the preimage of the ball. Their images are open because each is locally an inverse of a local diffeomorphism. Thus the ball is evenly covered. Every geodesic in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y\)</span>{% endraw %} also extends for all time by lifting its initial data to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %}, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y\)</span>{% endraw %} is complete.</p>
<p>Apply this to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p:(T_pM,h)\to M\)</span>{% endraw %}. A connected covering of a simply connected manifold has one sheet, by path and homotopy lifting. Hence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %} is bijective, and its local smooth inverses give a global diffeomorphism. Identifying {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T_pM\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb R^n\)</span>{% endraw %} finishes the proof. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<h3 data-note-section="1" id="application-estimates-on-geometric-quantities">1. Application: Estimates on Geometric Quantities</h3>
<div id="thm:bonnet-myers" class="theorem" data-note-number="3" data-note-kind="Theorem">
<p><strong>Theorem 3 (Bonnet–Myers).</strong> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a complete connected Riemannian manifold without boundary of dimension {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d\ge2\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{Ric}(X,X)\ge\lambda|X|^2\)</span>{% endraw %} for all tangent vectors, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, then</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\operatorname{diam}M\le\pi\sqrt{\frac{d-1}{\lambda}}.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Moreover {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is compact and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi_1(M)\)</span>{% endraw %} is finite.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Choose distinct points {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p,q\)</span>{% endraw %} and a unit-speed minimizing geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma:[0,\rho]\to M\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho=d(p,q)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Complete {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T=\gamma'\)</span>{% endraw %} to a parallel orthonormal frame {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T,X_2,\ldots,X_d\)</span>{% endraw %}, and set {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y_i(t)=\sin(\pi t/\rho)X_i(t)\)</span>{% endraw %}. These fields vanish at both endpoints. Minimality implies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(I(Y_i,Y_i)\ge0\)</span>{% endraw %}, even if the endpoint is conjugate. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y_i'=(\pi/\rho)\cos(\pi t/\rho)X_i\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[0\le\sum_{i=2}^d I(Y_i,Y_i)=\int_0^\rho\left[\frac{\pi^2(d-1)}{\rho^2}\cos^2\frac{\pi t}{\rho}-\operatorname{Ric}(T,T)\sin^2\frac{\pi t}{\rho}\right]dt.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> The {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_i\)</span>{% endraw %}, rather than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y_i\)</span>{% endraw %}, are orthonormal. Integrating sine squared and cosine squared gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho/2\)</span>{% endraw %} for each, hence</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[0\le\frac{\rho}{2}\left(\frac{\pi^2(d-1)}{\rho^2}-\lambda\right).\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> This bounds every distance and proves the diameter estimate. Hopf–Rinow makes the complete bounded manifold compact.</p>
<p>The connected universal cover with its lifted metric is complete: a geodesic projects to one in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}, which extends for all time and whose extension lifts from the prescribed initial point. This local isometry preserves curvature, so the same diameter estimate makes the cover compact. Each covering fiber is closed and discrete, hence finite in this compact space. The fiber of a universal cover is in bijection with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi_1(M)\)</span>{% endraw %}, so the group is finite. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<div id="prop:geodesic-existence-completeness" class="proposition" data-note-number="4" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 4</strong>. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a complete, connected, simply connected Riemannian manifold with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K_M\le0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma:[0,\infty)\to M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a unit-speed geodesic ray, and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\in M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Here a ray is globally minimizing: {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=|s-t|\)</span>{% endraw %}. There exists a unit-speed geodesic ray {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda:[0,\infty)\to M\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda(0)=p\)</span>{% endraw %} and</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d(\gamma(t),\lambda(t))\le d(\gamma(0),p)\qquad(t\ge0).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The simple-connectivity hypothesis makes this a statement on a Hadamard manifold; it is used for global distance comparison and minimality.</p>
<p>The same limiting initial vector works for all times: smooth dependence gives convergence uniformly on each compact time interval. No separate subsequence for each {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>{% endraw %} is required.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof of Proposition <a href="#prop:geodesic-existence-completeness" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:geodesic-existence-completeness">4</a>.</em> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_0 = d_M(p, \gamma(0))\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi: \widetilde{M} \to M\)</span>{% endraw %} be the universal covering map. Equipped with the pullback metric, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde{M}\)</span>{% endraw %} is a complete, simply connected Riemannian manifold with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K_{\widetilde{M}} \le 0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus, by the Cartan-Hadamard theorem (Theorem <a href="#thm:cartan-hadamard" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:cartan-hadamard">2</a>), {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde{M}\)</span>{% endraw %} is a Hadamard manifold and uniquely globally geodesic CAT(0) space. Because {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is simply connected, this connected covering is a diffeomorphism. With the pullback metric it is a global isometry. In particular, it is distance non-increasing: {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_M(\pi(x), \pi(y)) \le d_{\widetilde{M}}(x, y)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<p>Lift the given ray {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} to a geodesic ray {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde{\gamma}: [0, \infty) \to \widetilde{M}\)</span>{% endraw %}. Choose a lift of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, denoted {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde{p} \in \widetilde{M}\)</span>{% endraw %}, such that the distance realizes the minimum in the fiber: {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{p}, \widetilde{\gamma}(0)) = d_M(p, \gamma(0)) = d_0\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<p>For each integer {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i>d_0\)</span>{% endraw %}, let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde{\lambda}_i : [0, L_i] \to \widetilde{M}\)</span>{% endraw %} be the unique unit-speed minimizing geodesic from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde{p}\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde{\gamma}(i)\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(L_i = d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{p}, \widetilde{\gamma}(i))\)</span>{% endraw %}. By the standard triangle inequality on the vertices {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde{p}, \widetilde{\gamma}(0), \widetilde{\gamma}(i)\)</span>{% endraw %}, we bound the length: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[|i - L_i| = |d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{\gamma}(0), \widetilde{\gamma}(i)) - d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{p}, \widetilde{\gamma}(i))| \le d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{p}, \widetilde{\gamma}(0)) = d_0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<figure class="note-diagram" id="diagram-hadamard-asymptotic-ray" data-diagram="hadamard-asymptotic-ray" style="--diagram-width: 679px; --diagram-min-width: 509px">

<p>In a CAT(0) space, the distance function between two geodesics parameterized proportionally to arc length is convex. It need not be strictly convex: parallel Euclidean geodesics have constant separation. Consider the geodesics parameterized by proportion {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s \in [0, 1]\)</span>{% endraw %}, defined as {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s \mapsto \widetilde{\lambda}_i(s L_i)\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s \mapsto \widetilde{\gamma}(si)\)</span>{% endraw %}. By convexity, we have </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\begin{aligned}
    d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{\lambda}_i(s L_i), \widetilde{\gamma}(si)) &amp;\le (1-s) d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{p}, \widetilde{\gamma}(0)) + s d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{\gamma}(i), \widetilde{\gamma}(i)) \\
    &amp;= (1-s) d_0.
\end{aligned}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>For fixed {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, choose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i&gt;t+d_0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(L_i\ge i-d_0&gt;t\)</span>{% endraw %}, so both evaluation times below lie in the segment domain. Set {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s=t/i\in(0,1)\)</span>{% endraw %}; the convexity inequality gives</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d_{\widetilde{M}}\left(\widetilde{\lambda}_i\left(t \frac{L_i}{i}\right), \widetilde{\gamma}(t)\right) \le \left(1 - \frac{t}{i}\right) d_0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> Next, we evaluate the exact distance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{\lambda}_i(t), \widetilde{\gamma}(t))\)</span>{% endraw %} using the triangle inequality: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{\lambda}_i(t), \widetilde{\gamma}(t)) \le d_{\widetilde{M}}\left(\widetilde{\lambda}_i(t), \widetilde{\lambda}_i\left(t \frac{L_i}{i}\right)\right) + d_{\widetilde{M}}\left(\widetilde{\lambda}_i\left(t \frac{L_i}{i}\right), \widetilde{\gamma}(t)\right).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> Because {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde{\lambda}_i\)</span>{% endraw %} has unit speed, the first term on the right-hand side is precisely the absolute difference of the arguments: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\left| t - t \frac{L_i}{i} \right| = \frac{t}{i} |i - L_i| \le \frac{t}{i} d_0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> Combining these bounds, we obtain an upper bound for the distance at the same time {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>{% endraw %}: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{\lambda}_i(t), \widetilde{\gamma}(t)) \le \frac{t}{i} d_0 + \left(1 - \frac{t}{i}\right) d_0 = d_0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>We project this metric structure back down to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} by defining {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda_i = \pi \circ \widetilde{\lambda}_i\)</span>{% endraw %}. These are unit-speed geodesics from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(i)\)</span>{% endraw %} in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Because {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi\)</span>{% endraw %} does not increase distances: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d_M(\lambda_i(t), \gamma(t)) \le d_{\widetilde{M}}(\widetilde{\lambda}_i(t), \widetilde{\gamma}(t)) \le d_0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> The initial unit velocity vectors {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v_i = \lambda_i&#x27;(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} reside in the compact unit tangent sphere {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(S_p M \subset T_p M\)</span>{% endraw %}. We can pass to a convergent subsequence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v_{i_k} \to v \in S_p M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda(t) = \exp_p(tv)\)</span>{% endraw %} be the unique unit-speed geodesic from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} with initial velocity {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v\)</span>{% endraw %}. Because geodesic flows depend smoothly and continuously on initial conditions, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda_{i_k}(t) \to \lambda(t)\)</span>{% endraw %} for all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t \ge 0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Taking the limit as {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k \to \infty\)</span>{% endraw %} in the distance bound yields: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d_M(\lambda(t), \gamma(t)) = \lim_{k \to \infty} d_M(\lambda_{i_k}(t), \gamma(t)) \le d_0 = d_M(\lambda(0), \gamma(0)).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>It remains to check global minimality, which does not follow merely from being a geodesic. Fix {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\le s\le t\)</span>{% endraw %}. For all sufficiently large {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k\)</span>{% endraw %}, the segment {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde\lambda_{i_k}\)</span>{% endraw %} is defined up to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>{% endraw %} and is minimizing. Since the covering is a global isometry in the present simply connected setting,</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d_M(\lambda_{i_k}(s),\lambda_{i_k}(t))=t-s.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Passing to the limit gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_M(\lambda(s),\lambda(t))=t-s\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda\)</span>{% endraw %} is a ray starting at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, as required. ◻</p>
<p>The global comparison input used here is the convexity of distance between geodesics in a Hadamard space; see <a href="https://people.mpim-bonn.mpg.de/hwbllmnn/archiv/NPC0606.pdf">Ballmann, <em>Lectures on Spaces of Nonpositive Curvature</em>, Proposition I.5.4</a>. Cartan–Hadamard supplies this global setting from the hypotheses.</p>
