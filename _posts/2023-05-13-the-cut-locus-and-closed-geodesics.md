<div class="note-content" markdown="0">

<p>Throughout, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is a smooth, connected, complete Riemannian manifold without boundary. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, geodesics used to measure cut times have unit speed. Minimizing curves are unique only up to increasing reparametrization; uniqueness below means uniqueness of their constant-speed parametrizations.</p>

<div id="cut-points-and-cut-locus" data-note-section="title">

</div>
<p>The cut locus marks where radial geodesics stop minimizing. There are two possible mechanisms: the exponential map can become singular, or a second minimizing geodesic can reach the same endpoint. The distinction matters even in flat examples.</p>

<p>A geodesic ceases to minimize after its first conjugate point, but it can cease to minimize earlier. For the flat cylinder {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb S^1\times\mathbb R\)</span>{% endraw %}, the obstruction is angular winding, not height: a lift with angular displacement greater than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi\)</span>{% endraw %} has a shorter lift to the same endpoint. Vertical geodesics remain minimizing for all time.</p>

<div id="result-1" class="definition" data-note-number="1" data-note-kind="Definition">
<p><strong>Definition 1</strong>.  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((M,g)\)</span>{% endraw %} be a complete, connected differentiable manifold. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p \in M\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v \in T_p M\)</span>{% endraw %}. The <em>cut time of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((p,v)\)</span>{% endraw %}</em> is defined as </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[t_{\rm cut} (p,v) = \sup \left\{ t &gt; 0 : \gamma_v|_{[0,t]} \text{ is minimizing} \right\}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v\)</span>{% endraw %} is the maximal geodesic starting at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, with initial velocity {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<p>For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v\ne0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the definition allows any speed, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t_{\rm cut}(p,av)=t_{\rm cut}(p,v)/a\)</span>{% endraw %} for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v=0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the constant geodesic has cut time {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(+\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. In proofs using length equal to elapsed time, we normalize {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|v|=1\)</span>{% endraw %}. The minimizing times form an initial interval: a shorter path to an earlier point could be concatenated with the remaining segment to shorten any later segment.</p>
</div>
<p>A sufficiently short initial segment is minimizing by the normal-neighborhood theorem and Gauss's lemma, so the cut time is positive. Merely remaining inside an arbitrary ball does not imply minimality.</p>

<div id="result-2" class="definition" data-note-number="2" data-note-kind="Definition">
<p><strong>Definition 2</strong>.  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((M,g)\)</span>{% endraw %} be a complete, connected differentiable manifold. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p \in M\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v \in T_p M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t_{\rm cut}(p,v) &lt; \infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v (t_{\rm cut}(p,v)) \in M\)</span>{% endraw %} is called the <em>cut point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} along the geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v\)</span>{% endraw %}</em>. The <em>cut locus of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p \in M\)</span>{% endraw %}</em> is the set of all points {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q \in M\)</span>{% endraw %} such that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is the cut point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} along some geodesic, denoted {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{Cut}(p)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

</div>
<p>The cut locus depends on the global geometry. In the following models, explicit geodesics make it possible to identify the cut points directly.</p>

<div id="result-3" class="example" data-note-number="3" data-note-kind="Example">
<p><strong>Example 3.</strong> In Euclidean and hyperbolic space, every geodesic segment minimizes, so the cut locus is empty. On a positive-dimensional compact manifold, every unit-speed geodesic has finite cut time, since a minimizing segment has length at most the diameter; hence every point has nonempty cut locus. The dimension restriction excludes a one-point manifold.</p>
<p>On the unit round sphere {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb S^n\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\ge1\)</span>{% endraw %}, minimizing great-circle arcs have length at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}, and the cut locus of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\{-p\}\)</span>{% endraw %}. For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\ge2\)</span>{% endraw %}, this is also its first conjugate point. For the circle {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n=1\)</span>{% endraw %}, there are no conjugate points: an endpoint-zero tangential Jacobi field is identically zero.</p>
<p>On the flat cylinder, lift {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p=(e^{i\theta_0},z_0)\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q=(e^{i\theta},z)\)</span>{% endraw %} to the Euclidean plane. Geodesic lengths are</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\sqrt{(\theta-\theta_0+2k\pi)^2+(z-z_0)^2},\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> The least value is unique unless the angular difference is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi\)</span>{% endraw %} modulo {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(2\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{Cut}(p)=\{(e^{i(\theta_0+\pi)},z):z\in\mathbb R\}\)</span>{% endraw %}. Flatness makes every Jacobi equation {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(J''=0\)</span>{% endraw %} in a parallel frame, so there are no conjugate points.</p>
</div>
<p>The next theorem identifies what can happen at the first loss of minimality. Before that time, the geodesic is unique and has no conjugate point; at the cut time, singularity or nonuniqueness must occur.</p>

<div id="thm:cut-locus-properties" class="theorem" data-note-number="4" data-note-kind="Theorem">
<p><strong>Theorem 4</strong>.  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((M,g)\)</span>{% endraw %} be a complete, connected differentiable manifold. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p \in M\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v \in T_p M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c = t_{\rm cut}(p,v) \in (0,\infty]\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then,</p>

<ol>
<li><p>If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0 &lt; b &lt; c\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v|_{[0,b]}\)</span>{% endraw %} has no conjugate points. Furthermore, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v\)</span>{% endraw %} is the unique minimizing curve between its endpoints.</p></li>
<li><p>If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c &lt; \infty\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v|_{[0,c]}\)</span>{% endraw %} is minimizing, and one or both of the following conditions are true:</p>

<ol>
<li><p>{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v(c)\)</span>{% endraw %} is conjugate to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} along {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
<li><p>There are at least two minimizing geodesics from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v (c)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
</ol></li>
</ol>
<p>Here {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|v|=1\)</span>{% endraw %}. In the first assertion, there is no point conjugate to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p=\gamma_v(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;t\le b\)</span>{% endraw %}. Uniqueness concerns the unit-speed minimizing geodesic with the prescribed endpoints.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Write {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma=\gamma_v\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b&lt;c\)</span>{% endraw %}, choose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b'\in(b,c)\)</span>{% endraw %}. The segment to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b'\)</span>{% endraw %} minimizes. An interior conjugate point would give a negative index direction on this longer segment, contradicting the second variation necessary condition. If a second unit-speed minimizer {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\xi\)</span>{% endraw %} reached {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(b)\)</span>{% endraw %}, its terminal velocity would differ from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma'(b)\)</span>{% endraw %}, since equal terminal data determine the same geodesic backwards. Concatenating {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\xi\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma|_{[b,b']}\)</span>{% endraw %} gives a curve of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b'\)</span>{% endraw %} with a corner. In normal coordinates at that corner, replacing two short pieces by their chord shortens length: the old length is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(2\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %}, while the new length is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\varepsilon|u+w|+O(\varepsilon^2)&lt;2\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} for distinct incoming and outgoing unit velocities {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u,w\)</span>{% endraw %}. This contradicts minimality.</p>
<p>Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c&lt;\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. Continuity gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,\gamma(c))=\lim_{t\uparrow c}t=c\)</span>{% endraw %}, so the segment through {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c\)</span>{% endraw %} minimizes. Take {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b_i\downarrow c\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b_i&gt;c\)</span>{% endraw %}, and choose unit-speed minimizers {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_i:[0,a_i]\to M\)</span>{% endraw %} from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(b_i)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Hopf–Rinow supplies them. Their lengths satisfy {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a_i=d(p,\gamma(b_i))&lt;b_i\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a_i\to c\)</span>{% endraw %}. Compactness of the unit sphere at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} gives a subsequence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w_i=\sigma_i'(0)\to w\)</span>{% endraw %}. Hence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p(cw)=\gamma(c)\)</span>{% endraw %}, and the length-{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c\)</span>{% endraw %} segment with initial direction {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w\)</span>{% endraw %} is minimizing.</p>
<p>If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(cv\)</span>{% endraw %} is singular for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %}, the endpoint is conjugate to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}. Otherwise {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(cv\)</span>{% endraw %} is a regular <em>point</em> of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %}, with an injective neighborhood {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(U\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w=v\)</span>{% endraw %}, both distinct vectors {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a_iw_i\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b_iv\)</span>{% endraw %} eventually lie in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(U\)</span>{% endraw %}, yet their exponential images agree. They are distinct because their norms are {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a_i&lt;b_i\)</span>{% endraw %}. This is impossible. Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w\ne v\)</span>{% endraw %} and there are two distinct minimizing geodesics. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<p>We now sharpen the description by locating the first occurrence of either obstruction along the geodesic.</p>

<div id="prop:cut-point-conjugate-point-relationship" class="proposition" data-note-number="5" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 5</strong>.  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} be a geodesic on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(t_0)\)</span>{% endraw %} is the cut point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p = \gamma(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} along {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then, <em>at least</em> one of the following statements is true:</p>

<ol>
<li><p>{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(t_0)\)</span>{% endraw %} is the first conjugate point to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} along {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
<li><p>{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(t_0)\)</span>{% endraw %} is the first point along {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} such that there exists another geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma \neq \gamma\)</span>{% endraw %}, connecting {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(t_0)\)</span>{% endraw %}, with length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(L(\sigma) = L(\gamma|_{[0,t_0]}) = t_0\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
</ol>
<p>The geodesic is unit speed, and “another” means a distinct geodesic with its same unit-speed parametrization.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Theorem 4 already supplies one of the two obstructions at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t_0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Neither can occur earlier. An earlier conjugate point would lie strictly inside the minimizing segment {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([0,t_0]\)</span>{% endraw %}, and its Jacobi field would yield a shortening variation. An earlier second minimizing geodesic could be concatenated with the remaining segment to form a corner, which can be shortened as in the preceding proof. A second geodesic of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t&lt;t_0\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(t)\)</span>{% endraw %} is automatically minimizing because {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,\gamma(t))=t\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus the obstruction at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t_0\)</span>{% endraw %} is its first occurrence. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<p>Proposition <a href="#prop:cut-point-conjugate-point-relationship" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:cut-point-conjugate-point-relationship">5</a> carries two immediate consequences.</p>

<div id="result-6" class="lemma" data-note-number="6" data-note-kind="Lemma">
<p><strong>Lemma 6</strong>.  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a differentiable manifold, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q, p \in M\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q \notin \operatorname{Cut}(p)\)</span>{% endraw %}, then there exists a unique minimizing geodesic joining {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Completeness and connectedness give at least one minimizing geodesic by Hopf–Rinow. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p=q\)</span>{% endraw %}, the only minimizing curve is constant. Otherwise, two distinct minimizers would make either one nonminimizing immediately after {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, by the corner-shortening argument. Its cut time would therefore be exactly {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,q)\)</span>{% endraw %}, putting {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{Cut}(p)\)</span>{% endraw %}, a contradiction. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<div id="result-7" class="lemma" data-note-number="7" data-note-kind="Lemma">
<p><strong>Lemma 7</strong>.  If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q \in \operatorname{Cut}(p)\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p \in \operatorname{Cut}(q)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Reverse a minimizing segment {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma:[0,\ell]\to M\)</span>{% endraw %} from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to its cut point {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}. If there are two minimizers, reversing both gives two minimizers from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, and the corner argument prevents either from minimizing beyond {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}. Otherwise Theorem 4 gives a nonzero Jacobi field {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(J\)</span>{% endraw %} along {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} vanishing at both endpoints. The field {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\bar J(t)=J(\ell-t)\)</span>{% endraw %} is a Jacobi field along {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\bar\gamma(t)=\gamma(\ell-t)\)</span>{% endraw %}, also zero at both endpoints: the Jacobi equation is unchanged because both appearances of the tangent change sign. Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} is conjugate to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} along the reversed minimizing segment. Any extension beyond {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} has an interior conjugate point and cannot minimize. In either case the reversed cut time is exactly {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<p>In what follows, for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p, q \in M\)</span>{% endraw %}, let </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d_p (q) \triangleq d(p,q)\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> be the distance function from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} is fixed.</p>

<div id="prop:smoothness-distance-cut" class="proposition" data-note-number="8" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 8</strong>.  The function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} is smooth on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M \setminus (\operatorname{Cut}(p) \cup \{p\})\)</span>{% endraw %}. Furthermore, for each {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q \in M \setminus (\operatorname{Cut}(p) \cup \{p\})\)</span>{% endraw %}, if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma^q\)</span>{% endraw %} is the unique minimizing geodesic from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, then the gradient of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is given by </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[(\nabla d_p) (q) = \dot \gamma^q (d_p(q)) ~.\]</div>{% endraw %}
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\ne p\)</span>{% endraw %} lie outside the cut locus, let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r=d(p,q)\)</span>{% endraw %}, and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u=r\gamma'(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} be the initial vector of its unique unit-speed minimizer. This endpoint occurs before the cut time; otherwise it would itself be a cut point. Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(\exp_p)_u\)</span>{% endraw %} is nonsingular. Choose a local inverse near {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>We must check that nearby minimizing vectors use this same inverse branch. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q_i\to q\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u_i\)</span>{% endraw %} are any minimizing initial vectors with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_pu_i=q_i\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|u_i|=d(p,q_i)\to r\)</span>{% endraw %}. Every subsequential limit {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u_*\)</span>{% endraw %} satisfies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|u_*|=r\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_pu_*=q\)</span>{% endraw %}, so it is the vector of a minimizer to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}; uniqueness gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u_*=u\)</span>{% endraw %}. Consequently all such {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u_i\)</span>{% endraw %} lie in the chosen inverse neighborhood for large {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i\)</span>{% endraw %}. This proves that, on a neighborhood of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, the minimizer is unique and its vector is regular. Theorem 4 then excludes cut points in that neighborhood. It also shows that the domain outside {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} and its cut locus is open.</p>
<p>There {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p(z)=|\exp_p^{-1}z|\)</span>{% endraw %}, which is smooth since the inverse vector is nonzero. Given {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\in T_qM\)</span>{% endraw %}, choose a smooth endpoint curve {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma(s)\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma(0)=q,\sigma'(0)=X\)</span>{% endraw %}. The variation {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c(s,t)=\exp_p(t\exp_p^{-1}\sigma(s)/r)\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\le t\le r\)</span>{% endraw %}, consists of the minimizing radial segments. First variation gives</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d(d_p)_q(X)=\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_0L(c_s)=\langle X,\gamma'(r)\rangle.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\nabla d_p(q)=\gamma'(r)\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|\nabla d_p|=1\)</span>{% endraw %}. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<div id="result-9" class="remark" data-note-number="9" data-note-kind="Remark">
<p><strong>Remark 9</strong>.  The arguments above show that, if we appeal to the second variation formula, we can calculate the Hessian of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M \setminus (\operatorname{Cut}(p) \cup \{p\})\)</span>{% endraw %}. Furthermore, the arguments (almost) directly show that if there are two minimizing geodesics from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} cannot be differentiable at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}. An argument of this kind will appear in the next subsection.</p>

<p>Here is the nondifferentiability argument. For each minimizing geodesic to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, choose an interior point {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x\)</span>{% endraw %} sufficiently close to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_x\)</span>{% endraw %} is smooth near {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}. The smooth function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f_x(z)=d(p,x)+d(x,z)\)</span>{% endraw %} satisfies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\le f_x\)</span>{% endraw %} and equality at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} were differentiable there, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f_x-d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} would have derivative zero at its minimum, forcing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\nabla d_p(q)\)</span>{% endraw %} to equal that geodesic's terminal unit tangent. Distinct minimizing geodesics have distinct terminal tangents by ODE uniqueness, a contradiction.</p>
<p>For the Hessian away from the cut locus, let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\perp\gamma'(r)\)</span>{% endraw %}, and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(J\)</span>{% endraw %} be the unique Jacobi field with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(J(0)=0,J(r)=X\)</span>{% endraw %}. Differentiating the terminal radial tangent gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{Hess}d_p(X,X)=\langle J'(r),J(r)\rangle=I(J,J)\)</span>{% endraw %}, the last equality following by integration by parts and the Jacobi equation. The radial and mixed terms vanish because {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|\nabla d_p|=1\)</span>{% endraw %}. These identities underlie Hessian comparison.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-note-section="1" id="application-existence-of-closed-geodesics">1. Application: Existence of Closed Geodesics</h3>
<div id="thm:closed-geodesics-exist-cut-points" class="theorem" data-note-number="10" data-note-kind="Theorem">
<p><strong>Theorem 10</strong>.  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a complete differentiable manifold, and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p \in M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Suppose there exists a point {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q \in \operatorname{Cut}(p)\)</span>{% endraw %} such that the shortest distance from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{Cut}(p)\)</span>{% endraw %} is realized. Then,</p>

<ol>
<li><p>Either there exists a minimizing geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, along which {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is conjugate to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %};</p></li>
<li><p>Or there exist exactly two minimizing geodesics from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, denoted {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma\)</span>{% endraw %}. Furthermore, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma&#x27;(\ell) = -\sigma&#x27;(\ell)\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell = d(p,q)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
</ol>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell=d(p,q)=d(p,\operatorname{Cut}(p))&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. If any minimizing geodesic from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is conjugate at its endpoint, the first alternative holds. Otherwise all these minimizing vectors are regular points of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %}, and Theorem 4 supplies at least two distinct minimizers {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma,\sigma\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>Suppose their terminal unit vectors {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a=\gamma'(\ell)\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b=\sigma'(\ell)\)</span>{% endraw %} are not opposite. The vector {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v=-(a+b)\)</span>{% endraw %} then has {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\langle v,a\rangle=\langle v,b\rangle=-(1+\langle a,b\rangle)&lt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Choose a curve {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\tau(s)\)</span>{% endraw %} through {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} with velocity {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v\)</span>{% endraw %}. The two disjoint local inverse branches of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %} near {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\gamma'(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\sigma'(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} produce distinct geodesic variations ending at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\tau(s)\)</span>{% endraw %}. By first variation both lengths have negative derivative at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\)</span>{% endraw %}, so both are less than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %} for small {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>Every geodesic from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} of length less than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %} is minimizing: otherwise its first cut point would occur at a smaller length than the minimum distance to the cut locus. It follows that both varied segments minimize to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\tau(s)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Their lengths must agree with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,\tau(s))&lt;\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}; having two distinct minimizers makes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\tau(s)\)</span>{% endraw %} a cut point, again a contradiction. Thus any two distinct minimizers have opposite terminal tangents.</p>
<p>There are exactly two: a third would have terminal tangent opposite to the first, hence equal to the second, and uniqueness backwards from terminal data would identify those two geodesics. Their concatenation, with the second reversed, is smooth at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}. This gives a geodesic loop based at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}; smoothness at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} requires the extra argument in Proposition 11. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<div id="prop:closed-geodesics-either-or-curvature-bounds" class="proposition" data-note-number="11" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 11.</strong> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a complete connected Riemannian manifold of dimension {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\ge2\)</span>{% endraw %}, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;L\le K\le H\)</span>{% endraw %}. Write</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\iota(M)=\inf_{p\in M}\inf_{|v|=1}t_{\rm cut}(p,v).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> Either {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\iota(M)\ge\pi/\sqrt H\)</span>{% endraw %}, or there is a nonconstant smooth closed geodesic of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(2\iota(M)\)</span>{% endraw %}, and every nonconstant closed geodesic has at least this length. Equal-length shortest geodesics are allowed.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{Ric}\ge(n-1)Lg\)</span>{% endraw %}, Bonnet–Myers makes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} compact. We justify the attainment assertion used in the source. The cut-time function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c(p,v)\)</span>{% endraw %} on the unit tangent bundle is continuous. For upper semicontinuity, if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t&gt;c(p,v)\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,\exp_p(tv))&lt;t\)</span>{% endraw %}; this strict inequality persists for nearby {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((p,v)\)</span>{% endraw %}, so their cut times are less than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>{% endraw %}. For lower semicontinuity, suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((p_i,v_i)\to(p,v)\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c_i\to a&lt;c(p,v)\)</span>{% endraw %}. A uniform normal neighborhood near {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} makes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Theorem 4 says that each endpoint at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c_i\)</span>{% endraw %} is conjugate or has a second minimizing initial direction. A subsequence of conjugate cases would make {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(\exp_p)_{av}\)</span>{% endraw %} singular by continuity, impossible before the cut time. In the other case choose the second directions {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w_i\)</span>{% endraw %}. Compactness in a local tangent trivialization gives a subsequential limit {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w\)</span>{% endraw %}. The two limiting geodesics to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p(av)\)</span>{% endraw %} minimize, so uniqueness before the cut time gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w=v\)</span>{% endraw %}. But the map {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((x,u)\mapsto(x,\exp_xu)\)</span>{% endraw %} is locally injective near {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((p,av)\)</span>{% endraw %}; it cannot identify the distinct vectors {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((p_i,c_iv_i)\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((p_i,c_iw_i)\)</span>{% endraw %} converging there. This also is impossible. The same argument excludes any finite lower limit when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c(p,v)=+\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>The unit tangent bundle is compact, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;c(p,v)\le\operatorname{diam}M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus its minimum {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell=\iota(M)&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} is attained. Choose a minimizing pair {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p,q\)</span>{% endraw %} at distance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\ge\pi/\sqrt H\)</span>{% endraw %}, the first alternative holds. Assume {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell&lt;\pi/\sqrt H\)</span>{% endraw %}. No unit-speed segment of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %} has conjugate endpoints. Indeed an endpoint-zero Jacobi field has zero tangential part and index form zero, whereas the curvature upper bound and the one-dimensional Dirichlet inequality give</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[I(V,V)\ge\int_0^\ell(|V'|^2-H|V|^2)\,dt\ge\left(\frac{\pi^2}{\ell^2}-H\right)\int_0^\ell|V|^2\,dt&gt;0\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> for any nonzero endpoint-zero normal field. The scalar inequality follows by writing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f(t)=\sin(\pi t/\ell)u(t)\)</span>{% endraw %}, integrating the cross term, and obtaining {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\int(f'^2-(\pi/\ell)^2f^2)=\int\sin^2(\pi t/\ell)u'^2\ge0\)</span>{% endraw %}, first for compactly supported smooth {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %}, then by density; apply it componentwise in a parallel frame.</p>
<p>Theorem 10 therefore gives exactly two minimizers {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta,\xi\)</span>{% endraw %} from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, with opposite terminal velocities. Cut-locus symmetry and the global minimality of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %} show that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} realizes the nearest cut point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} as well. Apply Theorem 10 to the reversed segments, again with no conjugacy: their terminal velocities are opposite, which says {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta'(0)=-\xi'(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Concatenating {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta\)</span>{% endraw %} with the reverse of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\xi\)</span>{% endraw %} is thus smooth at both joining points and gives a closed geodesic of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(2\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>Finally, a nonconstant closed geodesic of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A\)</span>{% endraw %} supplies two distinct half-arcs from its starting point to its midpoint. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A/2&lt;\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}, both half-arcs would be minimizing and would remain before their cut times, contradicting uniqueness in Theorem 4. Hence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A\ge2\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}, proving the claimed shortest-length property. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
