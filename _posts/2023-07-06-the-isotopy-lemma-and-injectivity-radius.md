<div class="note-content" markdown="0">

<p>Throughout, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is a connected complete smooth Riemannian manifold without boundary. All minimizing geodesics used in distance arguments have unit speed. Compactness is stated where it is needed. The smooth-function results, Lemma 10 and Proposition 11, need only their stated compact-strip hypotheses.</p>

<div id="application-the-isotopy-lemma" data-note-section="title">

</div>
<p>Morse theory suggests following level sets by a flow, but the distance function is not smooth at the cut locus. To retain this approach, we use the directions of all minimizing geodesics in place of a single gradient. We apply this idea to the distance function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} (defined in Proposition <a href="{% post_url 2023-05-13-the-cut-locus-and-closed-geodesics %}#prop:smoothness-distance-cut" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:smoothness-distance-cut">8</a>). However, note that we cannot directly apply the theory, because—as we have seen in the proof of Proposition <a href="{% post_url 2023-05-13-the-cut-locus-and-closed-geodesics %}#prop:smoothness-distance-cut" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:smoothness-distance-cut">8</a>—{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} is not a smooth function on all of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Furthermore, outside of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{Cut}(p) \cup \{p\}\)</span>{% endraw %} and in neighbourhoods where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} is smooth, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|\nabla d_p| = 1\)</span>{% endraw %}, meaning there are no critical points to examine. Therefore, to apply the insights of Morse theory to the distance function, we need to adapt a few definitions.</p>

<div id="def:critical-point-dp" class="definition" data-note-number="1" data-note-kind="Definition">
<p><strong>Definition 1</strong> (Critical point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %}).  A point {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q \neq p\)</span>{% endraw %} is called a critical point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} if, for all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_q \in T_q M\)</span>{% endraw %}, there exists a minimizing geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q=\gamma(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} such that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\langle \gamma&#x27;(0), X_q \rangle \geq 0\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

</div>
<p>We regard {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} as a trivial critical point by convention; Definition 1 concerns {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\ne p\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathcal U_q\)</span>{% endraw %} be the compact set of initial unit directions of minimizing geodesics from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}. Noncriticality means that some outward vector {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(V\)</span>{% endraw %} satisfies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\langle V,u\rangle&lt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} for every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u\in\mathcal U_q\)</span>{% endraw %}; its negative is an inward vector with all inner products positive. Equivalently, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is critical if and only if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\)</span>{% endraw %} belongs to the convex hull of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathcal U_q\)</span>{% endraw %}, by finite-dimensional separation. A singleton direction set is not critical, so every nontrivial critical point lies in the cut locus.</p>

<div id="result-2" class="example" data-note-number="2" data-note-kind="Example">
<p><strong>Example 2</strong>.  Consider the distance function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} on the following manifolds.</p>

<ol>
<li><p>On {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb S^2\)</span>{% endraw %}, the only critical point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(-p\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
<li><p>On the cylinder {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb S^1 \times \mathbb R\)</span>{% endraw %}, the only critical point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((x, y)\)</span>{% endraw %} is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((-x, y)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
<li><p>On the flat torus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb S^1 \times \mathbb S^1\)</span>{% endraw %}, the critical points of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} are the two barycenters of the sides and the corner point.</p></li>
<li><p>On a compact manifold {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}, if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is a farthest point from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is a critical point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
<li><p>If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q \in \operatorname{Cut}(p)\)</span>{% endraw %} is not conjugate to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p, q) = d(p, \operatorname{Cut}(p))\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is a critical point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
</ol>
<p>These examples use the standard product metrics, with the base point itself omitted. On the sphere the antipode has all inward directions and is critical. On the cylinder, the two opposite angular choices can have convex hull containing zero only when their common vertical component is zero; this gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((-x,y)\)</span>{% endraw %}. On the square flat torus, represented by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([-\frac12,\frac12]^2\)</span>{% endraw %} centered at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, the distinct critical points are the two side-midpoint classes and the corner class: zero lies in the convex hull of the shortest lift vectors precisely at those points. The farthest-point assertion follows from the inward field argument below. The last assertion follows from the nearest-cut-point theorem: in the absence of conjugacy its two minimizing terminal directions are opposite.</p>
</div>
<p>The isotopy lemma uses this notion of critical point to identify an annular region with a product. Its proof constructs a vector field along which distance changes strictly.</p>

<div id="lem:isotopy-distance-function" class="lemma" data-note-number="3" data-note-kind="Lemma">
<p><strong>Lemma 3 (Isotopy lemma).</strong> If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;r_1&lt;r_2&lt;\infty\)</span>{% endraw %} and the closed annulus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A=\{r_1\le d_p\le r_2\}\)</span>{% endraw %} contains no critical point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %}, then</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[A\cong d_p^{-1}(r_1)\times[r_1,r_2],\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> with distance equal to the second coordinate. If the entire exterior {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\{d_p\ge r_1\}\)</span>{% endraw %} has no critical points, the corresponding conclusion is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p^{-1}(r_1)\times[r_1,\infty)\)</span>{% endraw %}; there is no level at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(+\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> The minimizing-direction sets are upper semicontinuous. Indeed, if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q_i\to q\ne p\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u_i\in\mathcal U_{q_i}\)</span>{% endraw %}, a convergent subsequence in a local orthonormal trivialization has limit {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u\)</span>{% endraw %}. Smooth geodesic dependence and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,q_i)\to d(p,q)\)</span>{% endraw %} show that the limiting segment reaches {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} with length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,q)\)</span>{% endraw %}; hence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u\in\mathcal U_q\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>At a noncritical {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, choose an inward vector {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_q\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\langle X_q,u\rangle&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} for all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u\in\mathcal U_q\)</span>{% endraw %}. Compactness of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathcal U_q\)</span>{% endraw %} gives a strict margin. Extend {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_q\)</span>{% endraw %} smoothly in a chart; the margin persists nearby by the preceding subsequence argument. A locally finite partition of unity combines these fields into a smooth field {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %} with the same strict inequality for every minimizing direction. Normalize it to unit length. On a compact annulus the margin has a uniform positive lower bound {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c\)</span>{% endraw %}, using compactness of the set of all minimizing directions over that annulus.</p>
<p>Distance need not be differentiable at a cut point, so we justify the monotonicity without choosing a smooth family of minimizers. Given {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u\in\mathcal U_q\)</span>{% endraw %}, choose a point {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z\)</span>{% endraw %} a short distance along that minimizer toward {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}. The smooth local function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h(y)=d(p,z)+d(z,y)\)</span>{% endraw %} lies above {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p(y)\)</span>{% endraw %}, agrees at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, and has {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\nabla h(q)=-u\)</span>{% endraw %}. Along an integral curve {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma\)</span>{% endraw %} of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %}, this bounds the upper right derivative of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p(\sigma(t))\)</span>{% endraw %} by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(-\langle X,u\rangle\le-c\)</span>{% endraw %}. Since distance is Lipschitz, its composition with this smooth curve is absolutely continuous, differentiable almost everywhere, and therefore</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d_p(\sigma(t_2))-d_p(\sigma(t_1))\le-c(t_2-t_1)\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> whenever the intervening trajectory lies in the compact annulus. This is the required strict decrease.</p>
<p>Hopf–Rinow makes the annulus compact. The flow exists while it remains there, and the displayed rate forces it to reach the lower boundary in finite time. Reversing the flow reaches the upper boundary. Each trajectory crosses each distance level exactly once. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\phi_t\)</span>{% endraw %} is the inward flow, define {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H(x,r)\)</span>{% endraw %}, for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p(x)=r_1\)</span>{% endraw %}, by following the reversed flow until its distance is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\)</span>{% endraw %}. The hitting time depends continuously on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((x,r)\)</span>{% endraw %}: continuous flow dependence gives convergence of distance functions, and the uniform strictly monotone rate bounds the change of hitting time by the change in the attained level divided by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c\)</span>{% endraw %}. The same argument for the inverse, which follows an arbitrary point to level {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r_1\)</span>{% endraw %}, proves that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H\)</span>{% endraw %} is a homeomorphism.</p>
<p>For an infinite exterior choose one locally finite field on the whole noncritical region. Every finite annulus has its own positive rate bound and compactness argument, so its trajectories reach each prescribed finite level. Unit speed prevents finite-time escape on a complete manifold. These compatible hitting maps give the product with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([r_1,\infty)\)</span>{% endraw %}. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<p>Thus topology cannot change across a compact interval of distance levels containing no critical points. The converse is not asserted: encountering a critical point need not force a change in topology, and a critical point need not be a farthest point.</p>

<div id="result-4" class="lemma" data-note-number="4" data-note-kind="Lemma">
<p><strong>Lemma 4</strong>.  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((M,g)\)</span>{% endraw %} be a compact differentiable manifold. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p \in M\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} be the farthest point from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} in terms of the usual distance function. Then, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is a critical point of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}. Furthermore, if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,q) = \operatorname{diam}(M)\)</span>{% endraw %}, then for any {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_p \in T_p M\)</span>{% endraw %}, there exists a minimal geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p=\gamma(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} such that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\langle \gamma&#x27;(0), X_p \rangle \geq 0\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> If a farthest point {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\ne p\)</span>{% endraw %} were noncritical, the reversed inward field just constructed would strictly increase distance from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} for a short time, contradicting maximality. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,q)\)</span>{% endraw %} is the diameter, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} is also a farthest point from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}. Apply the same argument to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_q\)</span>{% endraw %} at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, and unpack Definition 1 to obtain the stated inequality for every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_p\)</span>{% endraw %}. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<div id="result-5" class="lemma" data-note-number="5" data-note-kind="Lemma">
<p><strong>Lemma 5</strong>.  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((M,g)\)</span>{% endraw %} be a compact differentiable manifold of dimension {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p \in M\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_p\)</span>{% endraw %} has only one nontrivial critical point {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q \neq p\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is homeomorphic to the {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %}-sphere.</p>

</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Every farthest point is critical by Lemma 4. Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is the unique farthest point, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D=d(p,q)&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Choose a small normal radius {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r_0&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}. On {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\setminus\{p,q\}\)</span>{% endraw %}, construct one inward field as in Lemma 3. Its flow gives compatible product coordinates for all levels in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([r_0,D)\)</span>{% endraw %}: every finite subannulus is compact and noncritical. Attach this product to the normal ball at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\partial B_{r_0}(p)\cong\mathbb S^{n-1}\)</span>{% endraw %}, it follows that</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[M\setminus\{q\}\cong B_{r_0}(0)\cup_{\mathbb S^{n-1}}\bigl(\mathbb S^{n-1}\times[r_0,D)\bigr)\cong\mathbb R^n.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> The radial coordinate on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([r_0,D)\)</span>{% endraw %} can be stretched to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([r_0,\infty)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Finally, a compact Hausdorff space obtained by adding one point to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb R^n\)</span>{% endraw %} is its one-point compactification: complements of neighborhoods of the added point are compact, and every compact subset has a neighborhood of the added point disjoint from it. Hence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\cong\mathbb S^n\)</span>{% endraw %}. This also handles {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n=1\)</span>{% endraw %}; the hypothesis cannot occur for a connected zero-dimensional manifold. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<p>Before using the isotopy lemma in global comparison arguments, we describe the region where exponential coordinates are injective. Its size is measured by the injectivity radius.</p>

<h3 data-note-section="1" id="application-estimating-injectivity-radius">1. Application: Estimating Injectivity Radius</h3>
<div id="def:injectivity-radius" class="definition" data-note-number="6" data-note-kind="Definition">
<p><strong>Definition 6.</strong> The injectivity radius at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} is</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\iota(p)=\sup\{r&gt;0:\exp_p\text{ is defined and a diffeomorphism on }B_r(0)\subset T_pM\},\qquad \iota(M)=\inf_{p\in M}\iota(p).\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> For complete manifolds, the cut-locus theorem gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\iota(p)=\inf_{|v|=1}t_{\rm cut}(p,v)=d(p,\operatorname{Cut}(p))\)</span>{% endraw %}, with the empty-set distance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(+\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. Proposition 9 explains why “injective” gives the same definition in this setting.</p>
</div>
<div id="result-7" class="example" data-note-number="7" data-note-kind="Example">
<p><strong>Example 7.</strong> The unit round sphere has {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\iota=\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}: open radial balls of that radius map diffeomorphically onto the complement of the antipode. For the flat torus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb R^2/\mathbb Z^2\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\iota=\frac12\)</span>{% endraw %}. More generally, for a full lattice {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\Lambda\)</span>{% endraw %}, the value is half the shortest length of a nonzero lattice vector: distinct tangent vectors have the same exponential image exactly when their difference lies in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\Lambda\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus normalization of the torus metric matters. Euclidean space and the hyperbolic upper half-plane with metric {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((dx^2+dy^2)/y^2\)</span>{% endraw %} have infinite injectivity radius by their global exponential coordinates.</p>
</div>
<div id="result-8" class="remark" data-note-number="8" data-note-kind="Remark">
<p><strong>Remark 8.</strong> Every point has positive injectivity radius by a normal-neighborhood argument. A compact manifold has a uniform positive lower bound, using the inverse function theorem near its compact zero section (or the cut-time continuity proved in the previous note). Completeness and noncompactness alone do not determine the global infimum: Euclidean space has infinite injectivity radius and a standard flat cylinder has positive finite injectivity radius. It can also be zero, for example on a complete hyperbolic cusp quotient, where the nontrivial horizontal loops have lengths tending to zero toward the cusp. Lifting these loops to the hyperbolic plane shows that the shortest deck-displacement lengths, and hence the injectivity radii, tend to zero.</p>
</div>
<p>It is instructive to see the following equivalent formulation of the injectivity radius.</p>

<div id="prop:alt-def-injectivity-radius" class="proposition" data-note-number="9" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 9</strong>.  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a differentiable manifold, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p \in M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r &gt; 0\)</span>{% endraw %} be such that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %} is defined, and injective on the open ball {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B_r (0)\)</span>{% endraw %} in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T_p M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p|_{B_r(0)}\)</span>{% endraw %} is a diffeomorphism of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B_r(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} onto an open subset of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> We use the standing completeness assumption. Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(\exp_p)_v\)</span>{% endraw %} were singular at some {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v\in B_r(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}. It is nonsingular at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\)</span>{% endraw %}, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v\ne0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Choose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a&gt;1\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a|v|&lt;r\)</span>{% endraw %}. The segment {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(t)=\exp_p(tv)\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\le t\le a\)</span>{% endraw %}, has an interior conjugate point at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t=1\)</span>{% endraw %}, and therefore is not minimizing to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q=\gamma(a)\)</span>{% endraw %}. By Hopf–Rinow there is a minimizing initial vector {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|w|=d(p,q)&lt;a|v|&lt;r\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w\ne av\)</span>{% endraw %}, but {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_pw=\exp_p(av)\)</span>{% endraw %}, contradicting injectivity. Thus the differential is nonsingular throughout the ball. The inverse function theorem makes its image open and gives smooth local inverses; injectivity makes them agree on overlaps, producing a smooth global inverse. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<p>The following fact is proven in <span class="citation" data-cites="milnor_morse_1963">(<a href="#ref-milnor_morse_1963" role="doc-biblioref">Milnor 1963</a>)</span>.</p>

<div id="lem:proper-no-crit-point-deformation-retract" class="lemma" data-note-number="10" data-note-kind="Lemma">
<p><strong>Lemma 10 (Regular interval theorem).</strong> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f:M\to\mathbb R\)</span>{% endraw %} be smooth and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a&lt;b\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f^{-1}([a,b])\)</span>{% endraw %} is compact and contains no critical point, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^a=\{f\le a\}\)</span>{% endraw %} is a strong deformation retract of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^b\)</span>{% endraw %}. Explicitly, there are continuous maps {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_s:M^b\to M^b\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_0=\mathrm{id}\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_s|_{M^a}=\mathrm{id}\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_1(M^b)\subset M^a\)</span>{% endraw %}. The two sublevels are also diffeomorphic as manifolds with boundary. No nondegeneracy assumption outside the strip is needed.</p>
<p>In the proof below, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x_1=a\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x_2=b\)</span>{% endraw %}. The homotopy takes values in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^b\)</span>{% endraw %}, not in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^a\)</span>{% endraw %} at every intermediate time.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> First, we equip {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} with a Riemannian metric {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(g = \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle\)</span>{% endraw %}, which is always possible using a partition of unity. This allows us to define the gradient vector field {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\nabla f\)</span>{% endraw %} on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}, uniquely characterized by the relation {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\langle \nabla f, Y \rangle = d f(Y)\)</span>{% endraw %} for any smooth vector field {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y \in \mathscr{X}(M)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<p>Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K = f^{-1}([x_1, x_2])\)</span>{% endraw %}. By hypothesis, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>{% endraw %} is compact and contains no critical points of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus, the gradient {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\nabla f\)</span>{% endraw %} is non-zero everywhere on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>{% endraw %}, meaning {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lVert \nabla f \rVert^2 &gt; 0\)</span>{% endraw %} for all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x \in K\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<p>Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>{% endraw %} is compact and the set of critical points of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} is closed, we can find a relatively compact open neighborhood {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(U\)</span>{% endraw %} of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>{% endraw %} such that its closure {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\overline{U}\)</span>{% endraw %} also contains no critical points. On {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(U\)</span>{% endraw %}, we can define a normalized smooth vector field that points in the direction of steepest descent: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[V = - \frac{\nabla f}{\lVert \nabla f \rVert^2} ~.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> Next, we choose a smooth bump function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho : M \to [0,1]\)</span>{% endraw %} such that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho \equiv 1\)</span>{% endraw %} on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{supp}(\rho) \subset U\)</span>{% endraw %}. We then define a global vector field {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %} on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} by extending {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho V\)</span>{% endraw %} by zero: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[X(x) = \begin{cases} 
            \rho(x) V(x) &amp; \text{for } x \in U ~, \\
            0 &amp; \text{for } x \notin U ~.
        \end{cases}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> Because {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{supp}(\rho) \subset U\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %} patches smoothly to zero and is a globally well-defined smooth vector field on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Furthermore, since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %} has compact support (contained entirely in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{supp}(\rho) \subset \overline{U}\)</span>{% endraw %}), it is a complete vector field. This means it generates a globally defined 1-parameter group of diffeomorphisms {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\phi : \mathbb{R} \times M \to M\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\phi_t(x) = \phi(t, x)\)</span>{% endraw %} denote the flow of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %} at time {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<p>We now compute how the function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} evaluates along the integral curves of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %}. By the chain rule, </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\begin{aligned}
        \frac{d}{\dif t} f(\phi_t(x)) &amp;= d f_{\phi_t(x)} \left( \frac{d}{\dif t} \phi_t(x) \right) = d f_{\phi_t(x)} (X(\phi_t(x))) \\
        &amp;= \langle \nabla f(\phi_t(x)), X(\phi_t(x)) \rangle ~.
    
\end{aligned}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x \notin U\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X(x) = 0\)</span>{% endraw %}, so the derivative is zero. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x \in U\)</span>{% endraw %}, we substitute {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>{% endraw %} to get: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\frac{d}{\dif t} f(\phi_t(x)) = \left\langle \nabla f, - \rho \frac{\nabla f}{\lVert \nabla f \rVert^2} \right\rangle \Bigg|_{\phi_t(x)} = - \rho(\phi_t(x)) ~.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho \geq 0\)</span>{% endraw %} everywhere, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f(\phi_t(x))\)</span>{% endraw %} is non-increasing for all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t \geq 0\)</span>{% endraw %}. In particular, whenever the trajectory {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\phi_t(x)\)</span>{% endraw %} is inside {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>{% endraw %}, we have {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho(\phi_t(x)) = 1\)</span>{% endraw %}, yielding: </p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:flow-rate"></span><div class="math display">\[
        \frac{d}{\dif t} f(\phi_t(x)) = -1 ~.\tag{1}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> This means that as long as the flow remains inside {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>{% endraw %}, the value of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} decreases at a uniform rate of exactly {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1\)</span>{% endraw %} unit per unit time.</p>

<p>To construct the deformation retract, we want points in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^{x_2}\)</span>{% endraw %} to flow “downward” until they hit the boundary of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %}, and we want points already in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %} to remain stationary. Define a displacement time function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\tau : M^{x_2} \to \mathbb{R}\)</span>{% endraw %} by: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\tau(x) = \max(0, f(x) - x_1) ~.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} is continuous, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\tau\)</span>{% endraw %} is continuous. Note that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\tau(x) = 0\)</span>{% endraw %} if and only if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f(x) \leq x_1\)</span>{% endraw %}, i.e. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x \in M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<p>We now define the required homotopy {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_s : M^{x_2} \to M^{x_2}\)</span>{% endraw %} for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s \in [0,1]\)</span>{% endraw %} as: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[h_s(x) = \phi_{s \cdot \tau(x)}(x) ~.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> We can verify that this homotopy satisfies the defining properties of a strong deformation retract:</p>

<ol>
<li><p>At {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s = 0\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_0(x) = \phi_0(x) = x\)</span>{% endraw %}, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_0\)</span>{% endraw %} is the identity map on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^{x_2}\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
<li><p>For any {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x \in M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f(x) \leq x_1\)</span>{% endraw %}, meaning {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\tau(x) = 0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Consequently, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_s(x) = \phi_0(x) = x\)</span>{% endraw %} for all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s \in [0,1]\)</span>{% endraw %}. This proves that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %} is pointwise fixed by the homotopy, meaning {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_s|_{M^{x_1}} = \operatorname{id}_{M^{x_1}}\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
<li><p>At {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s = 1\)</span>{% endraw %}, let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h = h_1\)</span>{% endraw %}. We need to show that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h(M^{x_2}) \subset M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x \in M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h(x) = x \in M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x \in M^{x_2} \setminus M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x_1 &lt; f(x) \leq x_2\)</span>{% endraw %}, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x\)</span>{% endraw %} initially belongs to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>{% endraw %}. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} is non-increasing and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\frac{d}{\dif t} f(\phi_t(x)) = -\rho \geq -1\)</span>{% endraw %}, the function value {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f(\phi_t(x))\)</span>{% endraw %} can decrease by at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus, for all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t \in [0, \tau(x)]\)</span>{% endraw %}, we have {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f(\phi_t(x)) \geq f(x) - \tau(x) = x_1\)</span>{% endraw %}. This ensures that the trajectory does not leave {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>{% endraw %}. Hence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho(\phi_t(x)) = 1\)</span>{% endraw %} for the entire duration, and <a href="#eq:flow-rate" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:flow-rate">(1)</a> strictly holds. At time {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t = \tau(x) = f(x) - x_1\)</span>{% endraw %}, the function evaluates to: </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[f(h(x)) = f(\phi_{\tau(x)}(x)) = f(x) - \tau(x) = f(x) - (f(x) - x_1) = x_1 ~.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> Thus, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h(x) \in f^{-1}(x_1) \subset M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></li>
</ol>
<p>Therefore, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h = h_1 : M^{x_2} \to M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %} is a continuous map that restricts to the identity on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_s\)</span>{% endraw %} is the required continuous homotopy starting at the identity. This proves that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^{x_1}\)</span>{% endraw %} is a deformation retract of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^{x_2}\)</span>{% endraw %}. ◻</p>

<p>The same complete flow also gives the promised diffeomorphism. Its derivative satisfies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(-1\le(d/dt)f(\phi_t(x))\le0\)</span>{% endraw %} everywhere, and equals {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(-1\)</span>{% endraw %} in the strip. Consequently {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\phi_{b-a}\)</span>{% endraw %} maps {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^b\)</span>{% endraw %} into {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^a\)</span>{% endraw %}. Conversely, if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(y\in M^a\)</span>{% endraw %}, the reversed flow increases {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} by at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b-a\)</span>{% endraw %}, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\phi_{-(b-a)}(y)\in M^b\)</span>{% endraw %}. These inverse global flow maps restrict to a diffeomorphism of the sublevels. The fixed-sublevel deformation above is a different map and need only be continuous.</p>
</div>
<div id="prop:no-crit-point-homotopic-retract" class="proposition" data-note-number="11" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 11</strong>.  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a differentiable manifold of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\dim M = n\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f : M \to \mathbb R\)</span>{% endraw %} be a smooth function whose critical points are all nondegenerate. Given {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p, q \in M\)</span>{% endraw %} and a differentiable curve {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma : [0,1] \to M\)</span>{% endraw %} such that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(0) = p\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(1) = q\)</span>{% endraw %}, let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a = \max (f(p), f(q))\)</span>{% endraw %}, and set {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M^a = \{x \in M : f(x) \leq a\}\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b = \max_{t \in [0,1]} (f \circ \gamma)(t)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f^{-1}([a,b])\)</span>{% endraw %} is compact, and does not contain any critical points of index zero or one. Then, for all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\delta &gt; 0\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} is homotopic to a curve {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde \gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} such that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widetilde \gamma([0,1]) \subset M^{a+\delta}\)</span>{% endraw %}, where the endpoints are fixed.</p>

<p>The same conclusion holds for any {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a\ge\max(f(p),f(q))\)</span>{% endraw %}. The homotopy need only be continuous; its resulting path may be smoothed while staying below {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a+\delta\)</span>{% endraw %}, by first working below a slightly smaller level.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b\le a+\delta\)</span>{% endraw %}, use the original path. Otherwise choose a regular value {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a'\in(a,a+\delta)\)</span>{% endraw %}. The compact strip contains only finitely many critical points: their set is closed and nondegenerate critical points are isolated. Work in small disjoint Morse neighborhoods of these points and in compact regular strips between their finitely many critical values. Several points at the same value can be handled simultaneously; no uncontrolled perturbation of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} is required.</p>
<p>The regular-strip deformation pushes a path down between successive critical levels while fixing endpoints below {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a'\)</span>{% endraw %}. At an index-{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda\)</span>{% endraw %} critical point, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda\ge2\)</span>{% endraw %}, use the handle description in the preceding note. Its co-core {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\{0\}\times\mathbb D^{n-\lambda}\)</span>{% endraw %} has codimension at least two. A path can be perturbed, relative to its endpoints and outside the handle, to avoid this co-core: relative transversality makes the inverse image have dimension {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1-\lambda&lt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, hence empty. In the local coordinates this says {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u=x_-\ne0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Under the negative adapted flow, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u(t)=e^{2t}u(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v(t)=e^{-2t}v(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}, so</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[f(\Gamma_t(u,v))=c-e^{4t}|u|^2+e^{-4t}|v|^2\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> strictly decreases and reaches the lower regular level in finite time. Hitting times are continuous on the compact perturbed path; regular flow outside the chart completes the downward deformation. The same local construction works at a critical point initially lying on the top level, by first perturbing the path in its compact coordinate neighborhood. No global compactness above {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b\)</span>{% endraw %} is needed.</p>
<p>Inducting downward over the finitely many levels gives a path below {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a'\)</span>{% endraw %}, homotopic to the original relative to endpoints. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a'&lt;a+\delta\)</span>{% endraw %}, this proves the result. The argument constructs a deformation of the path; it does not assert that deleting an entire noncompact co-core automatically produces a global deformation retraction. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
</div>
<div id="prop:klingenberg-injectivity-radius-odd" class="proposition" data-note-number="12" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 12</strong> (Klingenberg).  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a simply connected, compact, differentiable manifold of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\dim M = n \geq 3\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %} is odd. Furthermore, suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\frac14 &lt; K \leq 1\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\iota (M) \triangleq \inf \{ \iota(p) : p \in M \} \geq \pi\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\kappa=\min K&gt;1/4\)</span>{% endraw %}. The index-form Dirichlet estimate under {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\le1\)</span>{% endraw %} excludes conjugate points on segments shorter than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\iota(M)&lt;\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}, the shortest-loop alternative proved in the preceding note supplies a nonconstant closed geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell=2\iota(M)&lt;2\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}, based at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>Choose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\varepsilon&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(5\varepsilon&lt;\ell\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(3\varepsilon&lt;2\pi-\max(\ell,\pi/\sqrt\kappa)\)</span>{% endraw %}, and with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(\ell-\varepsilon)\)</span>{% endraw %} nonconjugate to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} along the indicated segment. Such a choice exists because conjugate instants are finite on a compact interval. The local inverse of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %} at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((\ell-\varepsilon)\gamma'(0)\)</span>{% endraw %} supplies nearby long geodesics. Sard's theorem lets us choose a nearby endpoint {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} that is a regular value of the <em>whole</em> map {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %}. Choose it so close that the corresponding geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_1\)</span>{% endraw %} has length between {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell-2\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}, while {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,q)&lt;2\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %}, using the last short arc of the closed geodesic. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_0\)</span>{% endraw %} be a minimizer to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then the two geodesics are distinct and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(L_0=L(\gamma_0)&lt;2\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>We make the finite-dimensional Morse reduction explicit. Simple connectivity gives a homotopy of paths from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_0\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_1\)</span>{% endraw %}, fixing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p,q\)</span>{% endraw %}; choose it piecewise smooth with bounded energy {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E=\frac12\int_0^1|\dot c|^2\)</span>{% endraw %}. Fix {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(C\)</span>{% endraw %} above this bound and a uniform convex normal radius {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Subdivide {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([0,1]\)</span>{% endraw %} into {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(N\)</span>{% endraw %} equal pieces, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt{2C/N}&lt;r/2\)</span>{% endraw %}. An energy-{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\le C\)</span>{% endraw %} path has each subarc of length at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt{2C/N}\)</span>{% endraw %}, by Cauchy–Schwarz. Replace each subarc by its unique minimizing geodesic. This gives a continuous path in the finite-dimensional space of broken geodesics, described by their {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(N-1\)</span>{% endraw %} vertices, and does not increase energy. It fixes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_0,\gamma_1\)</span>{% endraw %} after they are given constant-speed parametrizations. The energy on vertices is</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[E_N(x_1,\ldots,x_{N-1})=\frac N2\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}d(x_j,x_{j+1})^2,\quad x_0=p,\ x_N=q.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Its sublevel {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E_N\le C\)</span>{% endraw %} is compact inside the domain where all adjacent distances are {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(&lt;r\)</span>{% endraw %}, because each adjacent distance is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(&lt;r/2\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is compact.</p>
<p>First variation says that a critical broken geodesic has matching velocities at every vertex, hence is a smooth geodesic. Its Hessian is the index form on piecewise Jacobi fields determined by the vertex values. Every endpoint-zero field splits as such a field plus one vanishing at every vertex; integration by parts makes these pieces index-orthogonal. The latter piece has positive index form on each short segment, by the index lemma. Therefore the finite-dimensional Hessian has the same index and nullity as the full geodesic index form. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is a regular value of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\exp_p\)</span>{% endraw %}, nullity is zero: {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E_N\)</span>{% endraw %} is Morse throughout this compact sublevel.</p>
<p>A geodesic of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(L&gt;\pi/\sqrt\kappa\)</span>{% endraw %} has index at least {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n-1\ge2\)</span>{% endraw %}. Indeed for parallel normal vectors {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(V\)</span>{% endraw %}, the fields {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y(t)=\sin(\pi t/L)V(t)\)</span>{% endraw %} satisfy</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[I(Y,Y)\le\left(\frac{\pi^2}{L^2}-\kappa\right)\int_0^L|Y|^2\,dt&lt;0,\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> and all their nonzero linear combinations satisfy the same inequality. Choose</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\max\{L_0,L(\gamma_1),\pi/\sqrt\kappa\}&lt;A&lt;2\pi-L_0.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Thus all critical points with energy {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(&gt;A^2/2\)</span>{% endraw %} have index at least two. Proposition 11 applied to the path in the finite-dimensional broken-geodesic space pushes it below {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((A+\eta)^2/2\)</span>{% endraw %}, for arbitrarily small {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, keeping its endpoints fixed. Choose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A+\eta&lt;2\pi-L_0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Cauchy–Schwarz now gives a homotopy from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_0\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_1\)</span>{% endraw %} through paths of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(&lt;2\pi-L_0\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>For clarity, the last obstruction is the long-homotopy lemma: a nontrivial geodesic digon cannot be contracted through loops all shorter than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(2\pi\)</span>{% endraw %} when the conjugate radius is at least {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}. Append the reverse of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_0\)</span>{% endraw %} to the preceding homotopy. Its starting loop is the retraced minimizer, which contracts without increasing length; its final loop is the nontrivial digon {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_1*\bar\gamma_0\)</span>{% endraw %}. All lengths are {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(&lt;2\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}, a contradiction.</p>
<p>Here is the lifting reason for that lemma. Parametrize a proposed contraction proportionally to arclength and split every loop into two half-arcs from its basepoint to its midpoint. Each half has length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(&lt;\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}, hence lifts under the nonsingular exponential map on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B_\pi(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}: Gauss's lemma bounds the radial norm of a lift by its travelled length, keeping it in a compact subball, so local inverses continue the lift. The two lifted endpoints agree for the constant loop; agreement is open and closed along the homotopy, because the exponential map is locally injective and the lifts vary continuously. They therefore agree for the digon. One half of the digon lies entirely in its longer geodesic side and lifts radially, with length equal to its endpoint radius. The other lift has the same endpoints and length, so equality in Gauss's radial length inequality forces it to be the same radial segment. This would make the digon retraced, contradicting the distinctness of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_0,\gamma_1\)</span>{% endraw %}. This is the argument of <a href="https://library.slmath.org/books/Book30/files/abresch.pdf">Abresch–Meyer, Lemma 4.3 and Remark 4.4, pp.21–23</a>.</p>
<p>The contradiction proves {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\iota(M)\ge\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}. The argument uses {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\ge3\)</span>{% endraw %}; the stated odd-dimensional case is the one needed here. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>
