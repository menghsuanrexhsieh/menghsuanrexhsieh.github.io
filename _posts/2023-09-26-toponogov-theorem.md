<div class="note-content" markdown="0">

<p>Throughout, manifolds and maps are smooth unless stated otherwise. On a Riemannian manifold, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\nabla\)</span>{% endraw %} denotes the Levi–Civita connection. The needed local constructions start with <a href="{% post_url 2022-02-05-differentiable-manifolds-charts-constructions-and-examples %}">coordinate charts</a>.</p>

<div id="toponogov-theorem" data-note-section="title">

</div>
<p>Rauch comparison controls infinitesimal separation along geodesics. Toponogov’s theorem gives a finite comparison: it relates the side lengths and angles of a geodesic triangle to those of a triangle in a constant-curvature surface. It extends the local estimates supplied by the Rauch comparison theorem (Theorem <a href="{% post_url 2023-08-16-comparison-theory %}#thm:rauch-comparison" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="thm:rauch-comparison">3</a>). We begin with a definition.</p>

<div id="def:geodesic-triangle" class="definition" data-note-number="1" data-note-kind="Definition">
<p><strong>Definition 1</strong>.  A <em>geodesic triangle</em> on a differentiable manifold {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is a set of three geodesic segments, each parametrized by arclength, denoted </p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\begin{aligned}
        \gamma_1 : [0,\ell_1] \to M ~,~ \gamma_2 : [0,\ell_2] \to M ~,~ \gamma_3 : [0,\ell_3] \to M ~,
    
\end{aligned}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> such that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_i (\ell_i) = \gamma_{i+1} (0)\)</span>{% endraw %} for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i=1,2\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_3 (\ell_3) = \gamma_1 (0)\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell_i + \ell_{i+1} \geq \ell_{i+2}\)</span>{% endraw %}. The endpoints of the geodesic segments are called the <em>vertices</em> of the geodesic triangle. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sphericalangle (-\gamma&#x27;_i(\ell_i), \gamma&#x27;_{i+1} (0))\)</span>{% endraw %} denote the <em>angle</em> of corresponding vertices {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i=1,2\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sphericalangle (-\gamma&#x27;_3(\ell_3), \gamma&#x27;_1(0))\)</span>{% endraw %} denote the <em>angle</em> of vertex {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i=3\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

</div>
<p>We use two formulations. The triangle formulation fixes three side lengths and compares angles. The hinge formulation fixes two side lengths and their included angle, then compares the distance between the remaining endpoints.</p>

<div id="thm:toponogov" class="theorem" data-note-number="2" data-note-kind="Theorem">
<p><strong>Theorem 2 (Toponogov).</strong> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a complete connected Riemannian manifold of dimension at least two, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\geq H\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M_H^2\)</span>{% endraw %} be the complete simply connected surface of constant curvature {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H\)</span>{% endraw %}, and put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_H=\pi/\sqrt H\)</span>{% endraw %} if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_H=\infty\)</span>{% endraw %} otherwise.</p>
<p><span id="thm:toponogov-a"></span><strong>(a) Triangle comparison.</strong> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((\gamma_1,\gamma_2,\gamma_3)\)</span>{% endraw %} be a geodesic triangle in the sense of Definition 1, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_1,\gamma_3\)</span>{% endraw %} minimizing. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, assume {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell_2\leq D_H\)</span>{% endraw %}. There is a comparison triangle in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M_H^2\)</span>{% endraw %} with the same side lengths, and its angles at the two endpoints of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\bar\gamma_2\)</span>{% endraw %} are no greater than the corresponding angles in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}:</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\begin{aligned}
\angle(-\bar\gamma_1'(\ell_1),\bar\gamma_2'(0))&amp;\leq\angle(-\gamma_1'(\ell_1),\gamma_2'(0)),\\
\angle(-\bar\gamma_2'(\ell_2),\bar\gamma_3'(0))&amp;\leq\angle(-\gamma_2'(\ell_2),\gamma_3'(0)).
\end{aligned}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>If all three sides minimize, the comparison holds at all three vertices. The triangle inequalities in Definition 1 are hypotheses when a side is not minimizing. For positive curvature, all sides here are at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_H\)</span>{% endraw %}. The comparison is unique up to a model isometry when no side has length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_H\)</span>{% endraw %}; antipodal and zero-side degeneracies are understood by limits.</p>
<p><span id="thm:toponogov-b"></span><strong>(b) Hinge comparison.</strong> Let a minimizing segment of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a\)</span>{% endraw %} join {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, and let a unit-speed geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma:[0,b]\to M\)</span>{% endraw %} start at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x\)</span>{% endraw %}, making angle {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} with that segment. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, assume {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b\leq D_H\)</span>{% endraw %}. Construct the model hinge with lengths {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a,b\)</span>{% endraw %} and angle {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d(p,\gamma(b))\leq d(\bar p,\bar\gamma(b)).\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> The second leg need not minimize. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, Bonnet–Myers already ensures {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a\leq D_H\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
</div>
<div id="result-3" class="remark" data-note-number="3" data-note-kind="Remark">
<p><strong>Remark 3.</strong> Part (a) fixes three side lengths and compares angles; part (b) fixes two lengths and their included angle and compares the opposite distance. These formulations are related by the model cosine laws. When {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_2\)</span>{% endraw %} does not minimize, part (a) compares the angles at its endpoints. The opposite angle is also compared when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_2\)</span>{% endraw %} minimizes.</p>
</div>
<div id="result-4" class="remark" data-note-number="4" data-note-kind="Remark">
<p><strong>Remark 4.</strong> The two segments and their angle in part (b) form a hinge. We first prove the comparison for sufficiently small hinges in normal neighbourhoods. To pass to an arbitrary hinge, we use Hessian comparison and upper supports for the distance function at cut points.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof of Theorem 2.</em> <strong>Local comparison.</strong> In a sufficiently small normal neighborhood of a hinge vertex, map the corresponding model configuration into {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} by</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[F=\exp_x\circ i\circ\exp_{\bar x}^{-1},\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> where the linear isometric embedding {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i\)</span>{% endraw %} identifies the two initial directions. The length form of Rauch comparison gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(L(F\bar c)\leq L(\bar c)\)</span>{% endraw %} for the model closing segment {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\bar c\)</span>{% endraw %}. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F\bar c\)</span>{% endraw %} joins the required endpoints,</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d(p,\gamma(b))\leq L(F\bar c)\leq d(\bar p,\bar\gamma(b)).\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> The normal neighborhoods must contain the entire closing segment, which is ensured by taking the configuration sufficiently small. This proves the local hinge statement. We now prove its global version without assuming a large configuration lies in one such neighborhood.</p>
<p><strong>A scalar form of Hessian comparison.</strong> For a curvature parameter {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k\)</span>{% endraw %}, define</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[h_k(r)=\int_0^r s_k(v)\,dv =\begin{cases}(1-\cos(\sqrt k\,r))/k&amp;k&gt;0,\\ r^2/2&amp;k=0,\\ (\cosh(\sqrt{-k}\,r)-1)/(-k)&amp;k&lt;0. \end{cases}\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_k'=s_k,\ h_k''=c_k=1-kh_k\)</span>{% endraw %}. It is strictly increasing for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;r&lt;D_k\)</span>{% endraw %}. Assume for the moment that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\geq k\)</span>{% endraw %} and, when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, that all distances under consideration are strictly less than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_k\)</span>{% endraw %}. At a point where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r=d(p,\cdot)\)</span>{% endraw %} is smooth, Hessian comparison gives</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\nabla^2r\leq\frac{c_k(r)}{s_k(r)}(g-dr\otimes dr),\qquad \nabla^2(h_k\circ r)\leq(1-kh_k(r))g.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Indeed, the chain rule contributes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c_k(r)\,dr\otimes dr\)</span>{% endraw %}, exactly completing the radial term. Along a unit-speed geodesic, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u(t)=h_k(d(p,\gamma(t)))\)</span>{% endraw %} therefore satisfies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u''+ku\leq1\)</span>{% endraw %} at smooth points.</p>
<p><strong>Cut points and upper supports.</strong> Fix {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z\neq p\)</span>{% endraw %} and choose a minimizing segment from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z\)</span>{% endraw %}, of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} be its point at distance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}. The function</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[r_\varepsilon(y)=\varepsilon+d(p_\varepsilon,y)\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> is an upper support for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,y)\)</span>{% endraw %} at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z\)</span>{% endraw %}, by the triangle inequality. It is smooth near {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z\)</span>{% endraw %}: the reversed segment from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} minimizes beyond {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %}, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} is before its cut time; symmetry of the cut relation then places {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z\)</span>{% endraw %} outside the cut locus of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %}. Hessian comparison at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z\)</span>{% endraw %}, now with radial distance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r-\varepsilon\)</span>{% endraw %}, gives for a unit vector {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(W\)</span>{% endraw %}, writing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v=dr_\varepsilon(W)\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\nabla^2(h_k\circ r_\varepsilon)(W,W) \leq c_k(r)v^2+ s_k(r)\frac{c_k(r-\varepsilon)}{s_k(r-\varepsilon)}(1-v^2) =1-kh_k(r)+o(1).\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Here {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|v|\leq1\)</span>{% endraw %} and the error tends to zero as {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\varepsilon\downarrow0\)</span>{% endraw %}. The increasing function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_k\)</span>{% endraw %} preserves the upper-support property. At {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} itself, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_k(d(p,\cdot))\)</span>{% endraw %} is smooth in normal coordinates: its power series is a smooth function of the squared radial norm, with Hessian {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(g\)</span>{% endraw %} at the origin. Thus every point of the parameter interval has smooth upper supports with second derivative at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1-ku(t)+\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %}, for every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>This support construction also appears in <a href="https://www.math.toronto.edu/vtk/eschenburg-comparison.pdf">Eschenburg, <em>Comparison Theorems in Riemannian Geometry</em>, §6, pp. 24–25</a>. The following one-dimensional argument spells out why it suffices here.</p>
<p><strong>From supports to the scalar inequality.</strong> On a compact parameter interval choose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(G\in C^2\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(G''=1-ku\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w=u-G\)</span>{% endraw %} has upper supports with second derivative at most any prescribed {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. This implies that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w\)</span>{% endraw %} is concave. Otherwise, on some {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([a,b]\)</span>{% endraw %}, it lies strictly below its endpoint chord at an interior point. For a small {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the quadratic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q(t)=\text{chord}(t)+\eta(t-a)(t-b)\)</span>{% endraw %} still lies above {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w\)</span>{% endraw %} somewhere. The negative minimum of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w-q\)</span>{% endraw %} occurs inside {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((a,b)\)</span>{% endraw %}. An upper support {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w_\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} there also makes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w_\epsilon-q\)</span>{% endraw %} attain a local minimum, forcing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w_\epsilon''\geq q''=2\eta\)</span>{% endraw %}, contrary to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon&lt;2\eta\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>Consequently {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w''\)</span>{% endraw %} is a nonpositive measure, and</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\mu:=u''+ku-1\leq0\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> in the distributional sense. The same concavity gives one-sided derivatives; they are finite here because the distance and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_k\)</span>{% endraw %} are locally Lipschitz. Variation of constants, valid also for this measure by integrating the scalar equation, yields</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[u(t)=u(0)c_k(t)+u'_+(0)s_k(t)+h_k(t) +\int_{(0,t)}s_k(t-v)\,d\mu(v).\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> One can verify the formula by differentiating distributions and its initial values; uniqueness of the homogeneous scalar equation then gives it. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s_k\geq0\)</span>{% endraw %} before {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_k\)</span>{% endraw %}, the measure term is nonpositive.</p>
<p><strong>Apply the initial data.</strong> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a=d(p,x)&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Use the chosen minimizing leg from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} in the upper-support construction at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x\)</span>{% endraw %}. The first variation formula gives</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[u'_+(0)\leq-s_k(a)\cos\alpha.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> The model function {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\bar u(t)=h_k(d(\bar p,\bar\gamma(t)))\)</span>{% endraw %} solves</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\bar u''+k\bar u=1,\qquad \bar u(0)=h_k(a),\quad \bar u'(0)=-s_k(a)\cos\alpha.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> These equations follow either from the constant-curvature Hessian identity or the model cosine laws; the modified distance is smooth even at its zero and, on the sphere, at the antipode. Therefore</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[u(t)\leq h_k(a)c_k(t)-s_k(a)\cos\alpha\,s_k(t)+h_k(t)=\bar u(t) \quad(0\leq t&lt;D_k).\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Monotonicity of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_k\)</span>{% endraw %} on the distance range gives the hinge inequality. The case {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a=0\)</span>{% endraw %} is simply {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(x,\gamma(t))\leq t\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H\leq0\)</span>{% endraw %}, take {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k=H\)</span>{% endraw %}. For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, take {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;k&lt;H\)</span>{% endraw %} and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k\uparrow H\)</span>{% endraw %}. Bonnet–Myers gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{diam}M\leq D_H&lt;D_k\)</span>{% endraw %}, while {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b\leq D_H&lt;D_k\)</span>{% endraw %}, so every preceding step applies, including the endpoint {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b=D_H\)</span>{% endraw %}. Model distances vary continuously with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k\)</span>{% endraw %}, proving (b) at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p><strong>Recover the triangle statement.</strong> Write {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a,b\)</span>{% endraw %} for the sides adjacent to a vertex, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c\)</span>{% endraw %} for the opposite side, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} for its actual angle. In a model hinge, the opposite distance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F_k(a,b,\theta)\)</span>{% endraw %} is continuous and strictly increasing in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;\theta&lt;\pi\)</span>{% endraw %} whenever the adjacent sides are nonzero and shorter than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_k\)</span>{% endraw %}. This follows directly from</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\begin{aligned} F_0^2&amp;=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos\theta,\\ \cos(\sqrt k\,F_k)&amp;=\cos(\sqrt k\,a)\cos(\sqrt k\,b) +\sin(\sqrt k\,a)\sin(\sqrt k\,b)\cos\theta &amp;&amp;(k&gt;0),\\ \cosh(\sqrt{-k}\,F_k)&amp;=\cosh(\sqrt{-k}\,a)\cosh(\sqrt{-k}\,b) -\sinh(\sqrt{-k}\,a)\sinh(\sqrt{-k}\,b)\cos\theta &amp;&amp;(k&lt;0). \end{aligned}\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> The triangle inequality and (b) give {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|a-b|=F_k(a,b,0)\leq c\leq F_k(a,b,\alpha)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus there is a model angle {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\bar\alpha\leq\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} giving the same third side. The three side lengths determine this model triangle up to isometry. If all three sides minimize, repeating the hinge comparison at each vertex gives all three angle inequalities for that one triangle.</p>
<p>For the more general triangle in (a), denote the vertices by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A=\gamma_1(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B=\gamma_2(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(C=\gamma_3(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}. At {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B\)</span>{% endraw %}, use the hinge consisting of the reversed minimizing segment {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_1\)</span>{% endraw %} and the geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_2\)</span>{% endraw %}. Its opposite distance is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(A,C)=\ell_3\)</span>{% endraw %}, because {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_3\)</span>{% endraw %} minimizes. The preceding argument constructs the model triangle and compares its angle at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B\)</span>{% endraw %}. At {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(C\)</span>{% endraw %}, use {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_3\)</span>{% endraw %} and the reverse of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_2\)</span>{% endraw %}; the opposite distance is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(A,B)=\ell_1\)</span>{% endraw %}. Part (b) now compares the angle at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(C\)</span>{% endraw %} in the same model triangle. At both vertices the possibly nonminimizing leg has length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell_2\leq D_H\)</span>{% endraw %} when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. At {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A\)</span>{% endraw %}, the opposite distance need not equal {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell_2\)</span>{% endraw %}, so this argument gives the third angle comparison only when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_2\)</span>{% endraw %} also minimizes.</p>
<p>For positive {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H\)</span>{% endraw %}, perform this construction at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k&lt;H\)</span>{% endraw %}. Normalize the first vertex and initial direction in the spherical models and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k\uparrow H\)</span>{% endraw %}. Compactness of the remaining initial directions gives a limiting triangle with the same side lengths and all the angle inequalities. This also handles antipodal or degenerate configurations, for which uniqueness may fail. No assertion of smooth dependence of ordinary distance at a coincident or antipodal endpoint is needed. ◻</p>
