<div class="note-content" markdown="0">

<p>Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_1,\ldots,X_n\)</span>{% endraw %} be i.i.d. from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F\)</span>{% endraw %}, with mean {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mu=\theta(F)\)</span>{% endraw %} and variance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;\sigma^2(F)&lt;\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. Write {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\hat F_n\)</span>{% endraw %} for the empirical distribution and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(P^*\)</span>{% endraw %} for probability conditional on the observations. The <a href="{% post_url 2023-03-04-bootstrap-distributions-and-consistency %}">setup and conditional convergence conventions</a> distinguish this conditional law from repeated sampling under {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>We first approximate the distribution of the unstudentized error. Its limit retains the unknown population scale:</p>
<span id="eq:root-t-statistic-nonparametric-mean"></span>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[R_n(X^n,\theta(F))=U_n=\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu),\qquad J_n(\cdot,F)=\mathcal L_F(U_n).\tag{1}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<div id="prop:bootstrap-consistency-nonp-mean" class="proposition" data-note-number="1" data-note-kind="Proposition"><p><strong>Proposition 1.</strong> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbf S_F\)</span>{% endraw %} consist of deterministic sequences {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F_n\rightsquigarrow F\)</span>{% endraw %} for which {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta(F_n)\to\mu\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma^2(F_n)\to\sigma^2(F)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p><span id="prop:bootstrap-consistency-nonp-mean-a" data-label="prop:bootstrap-consistency-nonp-mean-a"></span>(a) For every such sequence, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(J_n(F_n)\rightsquigarrow \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2(F))\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p><span id="prop:bootstrap-consistency-nonp-mean-b" data-label="prop:bootstrap-consistency-nonp-mean-b"></span>(b) For i.i.d. observations from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F\)</span>{% endraw %}, the conditional bootstrap law {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(J_n(\hat F_n)\)</span>{% endraw %} is strongly consistent. In particular, the cdf, quantile and coverage conclusions <a href="{% post_url 2023-03-04-bootstrap-distributions-and-consistency %}#eq:bootstrap-strongly-consistent">(2)</a>, <a href="{% post_url 2023-03-04-bootstrap-distributions-and-consistency %}#eq:bootstrap-quantile-consistent">(3)</a> and <a href="{% post_url 2023-03-04-bootstrap-distributions-and-consistency %}#eq:bootstrap-weakly-consistent">(4)</a> hold for each fixed probability level in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((0,1)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></div>
<div class="proof"><p><em>Proof.</em> In row {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %}, let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_{n,1},\ldots,X_{n,n}\)</span>{% endraw %} be independent with law {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F_n\)</span>{% endraw %}, and put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y_{n,i}=X_{n,i}-\theta(F_n)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y_{n,1}\rightsquigarrow X_1-\mu\)</span>{% endraw %} and its second moment converges to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma^2(F)\)</span>{% endraw %}. These two facts imply uniform integrability of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y_{n,1}^2\)</span>{% endraw %}, as justified in Lemma 4 below. For every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\varepsilon&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\mathbb{E}[Y_{n,1}^2\mathbf1\{|Y_{n,1}|&gt;\varepsilon\sqrt n\}]\longrightarrow0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>This is the Lindeberg condition at the correct {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %} scale. The triangular-array CLT proves (a). For (b), the empirical cdf converges weakly almost surely, and the strong law applied to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_i\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_i^2\)</span>{% endraw %} gives convergence of its mean and variance. Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\{\hat F_n\}\in\mathbf S_F\)</span>{% endraw %} almost surely. Apply Proposition <a href="{% post_url 2023-03-04-bootstrap-distributions-and-consistency %}#prop:bootstrap-consistency">3</a> of the preceding post. ◻</p></div>
<p>To remove the unknown scale from the limiting law, estimate it in the same sample as the numerator. Throughout this post use the empirical variance with divisor {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %}:</p>
<span id="eq:root-studentized-t-statistic-nonparametric-mean"></span>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\hat\sigma_n^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X_n)^2,\qquad R_n^{\rm student}=T_n=\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\hat\sigma_n},\qquad K_n(\cdot,F)=\mathcal L_F(T_n).\tag{2}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Define the ratio arbitrarily, say as zero, when the denominator is zero; the probability of this event tends to zero under the assumptions here. The bootstrap recomputes both the mean and the standard deviation in each resample. To justify this step along changing populations, we need a weak law for triangular arrays.</p>
<div id="lem:wlln-triangular-array" class="lemma" data-note-number="2" data-note-kind="Lemma"><p><strong>Lemma 2.</strong> Suppose each row {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y_{n,1},\ldots,Y_{n,n}\)</span>{% endraw %} is i.i.d., {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y_{n,1}\rightsquigarrow Y\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{E}|Y_{n,1}|\to \mathbb{E}|Y|&lt;\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n^{-1}\sum_iY_{n,i}\to \mathbb{E}Y\)</span>{% endraw %} in probability.</p></div>
<p>The full Lindeberg normalization in Proposition 1 is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s_n^2=n\sigma_n^2\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_n^2=\operatorname{Var}_{F_n}(X)\to\sigma^2&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\frac1{s_n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n E[Y_{n,i}^2\mathbf1\{|Y_{n,i}|&gt;\varepsilon s_n\}]=\frac1{\sigma_n^2}E[Y_{n,1}^2\mathbf1\{|Y_{n,1}|&gt;\varepsilon\sqrt n\sigma_n\}]\longrightarrow0.\]</div>{% endraw %}

<p>The variance stays bounded away from zero, and the threshold tends to infinity, so uniform integrability is exactly the required tail control. The CLT gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\theta(F_n))/\sigma_n\Rightarrow \mathcal{N}(0,1)\)</span>{% endraw %}; multiplying by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_n\to\sigma\)</span>{% endraw %} gives the unstudentized conclusion.</p>
<p>The proof separates two tasks: control the tails uniformly, then apply a weak law to bounded truncations.</p>
<div id="lem:triangular-array-mean-converges" class="lemma" data-note-number="3" data-note-kind="Lemma"><p><strong>Lemma 3.</strong> Suppose each row is i.i.d. with finite mean {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mu_n\)</span>{% endraw %}, and</p>
<span id="eq:random-aui-condition"></span>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\lim_{A\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}E[|Y_{n,1}-\mu_n|\mathbf1\{|Y_{n,1}-\mu_n|&gt;A\}]=0.\tag{3}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\bar Y_n-\mu_n\to0\)</span>{% endraw %} in probability.</p></div>
<div class="proof"><p><em>Proof.</em> Center the variables and write {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Z_{n,i}=Y_{n,i}-\mu_n\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Z_{n,i}^{A}=Z_{n,i}\mathbf1\{|Z_{n,i}|\le A\}\)</span>{% endraw %}. For fixed {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A\)</span>{% endraw %}, independence and Chebyshev give</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[P\!\left(\left|\frac1n\sum_i(Z_{n,i}^{A}-\mathbb{E}Z_{n,i}^{A})\right|&gt;\varepsilon\right)\le\frac{A^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\longrightarrow0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The expected absolute difference between the centered truncated average and the original average is at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(2\mathbb{E}[|Z_{n,1}|\mathbf1\{|Z_{n,1}|&gt;A\}]\)</span>{% endraw %}. Markov's inequality, followed first by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\to\infty\)</span>{% endraw %} and then by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A\to\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}, completes the proof. ◻</p></div>
<p>Writing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T_{n,i}^A=Z_{n,i}\mathbf1\{|Z_{n,i}|&gt;A\}\)</span>{% endraw %}, the difference of averages in Lemma 3 is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n^{-1}\sum_i(T_{n,i}^A-ET_{n,i}^A)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Its absolute expectation is at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(2E|T_{n,1}^A|\)</span>{% endraw %}. Consequently the complete probability bound is</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[P(|\bar Z_n|&gt;2\varepsilon)\le\frac{A^2}{n\varepsilon^2}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon}E[|Z_{n,1}|\mathbf1\{|Z_{n,1}|&gt;A\}].\]</div>{% endraw %}

<p>The order of limits matters: first let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\to\infty\)</span>{% endraw %} at fixed {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A\)</span>{% endraw %}, and only then let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A\to\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. No second-moment assumption on the untruncated variables is used.</p>
<div id="lem:convergence-in-distribution-almost-implies-convergence-in-moments" class="lemma" data-note-number="4" data-note-kind="Lemma"><p><strong>Lemma 4.</strong> Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_n\rightsquigarrow X\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p><span id="lem:convergence-in-distribution-almost-implies-convergence-in-moments-a" data-label="lem:convergence-in-distribution-almost-implies-convergence-in-moments-a"></span>(a) If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|X_n|\le M\)</span>{% endraw %} almost surely for one finite {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{E}X_n\to \mathbb{E}X\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p><span id="lem:convergence-in-distribution-almost-implies-convergence-in-moments-b" data-label="lem:convergence-in-distribution-almost-implies-convergence-in-moments-b"></span>(b) At every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(P(|X|=A)=0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the expectations of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|X_n|\mathbf1\{|X_n|\le A\}\)</span>{% endraw %} converge. If each variable is integrable and also {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{E}|X_n|\to \mathbb{E}|X|&lt;\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\{|X_n|\}\)</span>{% endraw %} is uniformly integrable and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{E}X_n\to \mathbb{E}X\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p></div>
<div class="proof"><p><em>Proof.</em> Bounded continuous test functions prove (a); for (b), the bounded truncation has discontinuities only at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pm A\)</span>{% endraw %}, which have zero limiting probability. Subtracting its expectation from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{E}|X_n|\)</span>{% endraw %} gives convergence of the tail expectations at such {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let these continuity points increase to infinity. The finitely many early indices have integrable tails too, so the resulting bound is uniform in all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %}. Truncating the signed variables then proves convergence of their means. ◻</p></div>
<div class="proof"><p><em>Proof of Lemma 2.</em> Lemma 4 gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{E}Y_{n,1}\to \mathbb{E}Y\)</span>{% endraw %} and uniform integrability. Since the means are bounded, centering preserves the tail condition (3). Lemma 3 now proves the conclusion. ◻</p></div>
<div id="prop:bootstrap-consistency-nonp-mean-studentized" class="proposition" data-note-number="5" data-note-kind="Proposition"><p><strong>Proposition 5.</strong> Under the assumptions of Proposition 1:</p>
<p><span id="prop:bootstrap-consistency-nonp-mean-studentized-a" data-label="prop:bootstrap-consistency-nonp-mean-studentized-a"></span>(a) {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K_n(F_n)\rightsquigarrow \mathcal{N}(0,1)\)</span>{% endraw %} for every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\{F_n\}\in\mathbf S_F\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p><span id="prop:bootstrap-consistency-nonp-mean-studentized-b" data-label="prop:bootstrap-consistency-nonp-mean-studentized-b"></span>(b) The conditional studentized bootstrap law {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K_n(\hat F_n)\)</span>{% endraw %} is strongly consistent, with the same cdf, quantile and coverage conclusions as in Proposition 1.</p></div>
<div class="proof"><p><em>Proof.</em> The assumptions on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F_n\)</span>{% endraw %} give convergence of the first two moments. Lemma 4 makes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_{n,1}^2\)</span>{% endraw %} uniformly integrable, and hence makes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|X_{n,1}|\)</span>{% endraw %} uniformly integrable as well. Apply Lemma 2 to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_{n,i}\)</span>{% endraw %} and to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_{n,i}^2\)</span>{% endraw %}:</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\frac1n\sum_i(X_{n,i}-\bar X_n)^2=\frac1n\sum_iX_{n,i}^2-\bar X_n^2\ \xrightarrow{P}\ \sigma^2(F)&gt;0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Combine this with Proposition 1 and Slutsky's theorem. The almost-sure empirical-distribution argument used there also proves (b). ◻</p></div>

<h3 data-note-section="1" id="bootstrap-coverage-errors">1. Bootstrap Coverage Errors</h3>
<p>First-order consistency gives limiting coverage, but does not distinguish the error rates of confidence intervals. Edgeworth expansions describe the first departure from normality. Put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^3]/\sigma^3\)</span>{% endraw %}, and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\Phi,\phi\)</span>{% endraw %} be the standard normal cdf and density.</p>
<div id="thm:edgeworth-expansion-t-test" class="theorem" data-note-number="6" data-note-kind="Theorem"><p><strong>Theorem 6.</strong> If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{E}|X|^4&lt;\infty\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\limsup_{|t|\to\infty}|Ee^{itX}|&lt;1\)</span>{% endraw %}, then, uniformly in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[J_n(\sigma x,F)=\Phi(x)+n^{-1/2}p_1(x)\phi(x)+O(n^{-1}),\qquad p_1(x)=\frac\gamma6(1-x^2).\]</div>{% endraw %}
</div>
<div id="thm:edgeworth-expansion-studentized-t-test" class="theorem" data-note-number="7" data-note-kind="Theorem"><p><strong>Theorem 7.</strong> If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{E}|X|^8&lt;\infty\)</span>{% endraw %} and the vector {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((X,X^2)\)</span>{% endraw %} satisfies Cramér's condition, then, uniformly in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[K_n(x,F)=\Phi(x)+n^{-1/2}q_1(x)\phi(x)+O(n^{-1}),\qquad q_1(x)=\frac\gamma6(2x^2+1).\]</div>{% endraw %}
</div>
<p>These are applications of the smooth-function Edgeworth theorem in Hall, <a href="https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jordan/sail/readings/edgeworth.pdf"><em>The Bootstrap and Edgeworth Expansion</em></a>, Theorem 2.2 and the mean example in Section 2.4. For Theorem 7 take the sample-mean vector of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((X,X^2)\)</span>{% endraw %} and the smooth map {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((a,b)\mapsto(a-\mu)/(b-a^2)^{1/2}\)</span>{% endraw %}, in a neighborhood of the population moments where the denominator is positive. The moment assumption supplies the fourth moment of this vector. The displayed coefficients use the divisor-{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %} variance fixed above.</p>
<p>For the bootstrap coverage comparisons below, impose the following sufficient conditions: {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{E}|X|^r&lt;\infty\)</span>{% endraw %} for every positive integer {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\)</span>{% endraw %}, and, for each such {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\limsup_{\|t\|\to\infty}\left|E\exp\!\left(i\sum_{j=1}^r t_jX^j\right)\right|&lt;1.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>These explicit assumptions allow the joint expansions of every finite vector of empirical moments needed below. They are stronger than the finite-variance assumptions for consistency. We use Hall's Theorems 5.1–5.2 for conditional cdfs and quantiles, and Section 5.3 for coverage probabilities with estimated critical values. In particular, the coverage claims use those joint expansions, rather than replacing an {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(O_P(n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %} error inside an event by an {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(O(n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %} error in probability. All rates below are for fixed {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;\alpha&lt;1/2\)</span>{% endraw %}, or uniformly over a compact subinterval of this range.</p>
<p><strong>Normal intervals.</strong> The upper confidence bound is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(U_N=\bar X_n+\hat\sigma_n z_{1-\alpha}/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %}. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mu\le U_N\)</span>{% endraw %} is the event {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T_n\ge z_\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}, Theorem 7 gives</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[P_F(\mu\le U_N)-(1-\alpha)=-\frac{\gamma}{6\sqrt n}(2z_\alpha^2+1)\phi(z_\alpha)+O(n^{-1}).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>For the symmetric interval {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(I_N=[\bar X_n\pm\hat\sigma_n z_{1-\alpha}/\sqrt n]\)</span>{% endraw %}, the two cdf corrections cancel: {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q_1\phi\)</span>{% endraw %} is even, so</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[P_F(\mu\in I_N)=K_n(z_{1-\alpha},F)-K_n((-z_{1-\alpha})-,F)=1-2\alpha+O(n^{-1}).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Here the minus after the lower endpoint denotes the left limit of the cdf. The uniform continuous Edgeworth approximation bounds any jump by its remainder order, so the same expansion applies at that left limit. Thus two-sided normal coverage is already accurate to order {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n^{-1}\)</span>{% endraw %}. This cancellation does not say that the errors in the two individual tails are of that order.</p>
<p>The inversion step can be checked before considering random critical values. Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H_n(x)=\Phi(x)+n^{-1/2}a(x)\phi(x)+O(n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %} uniformly near {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z=z_\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a\)</span>{% endraw %} smooth. Set {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x_n=z+n^{-1/2}b\)</span>{% endraw %}. Taylor expansion gives</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[H_n(x_n)=\alpha+n^{-1/2}\{b+a(z)\}\phi(z)+O(n^{-1}).\]</div>{% endraw %}

<p>Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\phi(z)&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, choose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b=-a(z)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Evaluating at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x_n\pm C/n\)</span>{% endraw %} for a sufficiently large constant traps the generalized inverse, giving {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H_n^{-1}(\alpha)=z-n^{-1/2}a(z)+O(n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %}. The same trapping works in probability for the conditional cdf and estimated coefficients. This derives the signs in both bootstrap quantile formulas below; the coverage remainder still requires the separate joint expansion already specified.</p>
<p><strong>The unstudentized bootstrap.</strong> Put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\hat\gamma=\gamma(\hat F_n)\)</span>{% endraw %}. The conditional expansion and its inverse are</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[J_n(\hat\sigma_n x,\hat F_n)=\Phi(x)+\frac{\hat\gamma}{6\sqrt n}(1-x^2)\phi(x)+O_P(n^{-1}),\]</div>{% endraw %}
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\frac{J_n^{-1}(\alpha,\hat F_n)}{\hat\sigma_n}=z_\alpha+\frac{\hat\gamma}{6\sqrt n}(z_\alpha^2-1)+O_P(n^{-1}).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Here the upper basic-bootstrap bound is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(U_B=\bar X_n-J_n^{-1}(\alpha,\hat F_n)/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %}. Hall's coverage expansion (3.39), justified in Section 5.3, applies to the smooth empirical-moment coefficients in this quantile. Its first correction is obtained by adding the quantile shift {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(z_\alpha^2-1)/6\)</span>{% endraw %} to the studentized cdf correction {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q_1(z_\alpha)\)</span>{% endraw %}:</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[P_F(\mu\le U_B)-(1-\alpha)=-\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt n}z_\alpha^2\phi(z_\alpha)+O(n^{-1}).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The random estimation of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} enters at the next order in that joint expansion. There is no general ordering between this error constant and the normal-interval constant. The equal-tailed basic interval has endpoints</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\left[\bar X_n-\frac{J_n^{-1}(1-\alpha,\hat F_n)}{\sqrt n},\ \bar X_n-\frac{J_n^{-1}(\alpha,\hat F_n)}{\sqrt n}\right].\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Its coverage is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1-2\alpha+O(n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %}, by cancellation of the even first correction.</p>
<p>In the basic-bootstrap coverage calculation the first coefficient is</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\frac\gamma6(z_\alpha^2-1)+q_1(z_\alpha)=\frac\gamma6\{z_\alpha^2-1+2z_\alpha^2+1\}=\frac\gamma2z_\alpha^2.\]</div>{% endraw %}

<p>Coverage of an upper bound is one minus the lower-tail probability, so this coefficient appears with a minus sign. For an equal-tailed interval the same expression, multiplied by the even density {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\phi\)</span>{% endraw %}, takes equal values at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z_\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(-z_\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}; subtracting the two cdf values removes the entire {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n^{-1/2}\)</span>{% endraw %} term.</p>
<p><strong>The studentized bootstrap.</strong> The corresponding expansion is</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[K_n^{-1}(\alpha,\hat F_n)=z_\alpha-\frac{\hat\gamma}{6\sqrt n}(2z_\alpha^2+1)+O_P(n^{-1}).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(U_t=\bar X_n-\hat\sigma_nK_n^{-1}(\alpha,\hat F_n)/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %}, the first quantile correction is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(-q_1(z_\alpha)\)</span>{% endraw %}. It cancels the first cdf correction in the same coverage theorem, giving</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[P_F(\mu\le U_t)=1-\alpha+O(n^{-1}).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The equal-tailed interval uses the {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1-\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} quantiles in its lower and upper endpoints respectively, both with a minus sign; its coverage is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1-2\alpha+O(n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %}. Studentization improves one-sided coverage because the normal limit no longer contains an unknown scale.</p>
<p>A different construction uses the absolute studentized root, with both the absolute value and resampled denominator included:</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[|T_n|=\frac{\sqrt n\,|\bar X_n-\mu|}{\hat\sigma_n},\qquad c_n^*=\inf\{c:P^*(|T_n^*|\le c)\ge1-2\alpha\}.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Under the same sufficient conditions, the symmetric bootstrap interval {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([\bar X_n\pm\hat\sigma_nc_n^*/\sqrt n]\)</span>{% endraw %} has coverage {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1-2\alpha+O(n^{-2})\)</span>{% endraw %}, by Hall, Section 3.6, equations (3.53)–(3.58), with the remainder justification of Sections 5.2–5.3. In that argument the even first correction cancels from the distribution of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|T_n|\)</span>{% endraw %}; estimating its next correction changes the critical value by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(O_P(n^{-3/2})\)</span>{% endraw %}, and the odd-order term in the <em>joint coverage expansion</em> cancels between the two tails. The last cancellation, with the stated remainder control, is what gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n^{-2}\)</span>{% endraw %}; critical-value accuracy alone would not establish it.</p>
