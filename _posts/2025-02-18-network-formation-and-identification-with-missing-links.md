<div class="note-content" markdown="0">

<p>If people choose their neighbors, a link reflects incentives as well as a channel for spillovers. When links are unobserved, an outcome equation alone generally cannot recover the network. Identification then depends on restrictions on how links form, how outcomes respond to them, and which variables are observed.</p>

<p>The exposition below is based on <span class="citation" data-cites="leung_two-step_2015">Leung (<a href="#ref-leung_two-step_2015" role="doc-biblioref">2015</a>)</span>, <span class="citation" data-cites="battaglini_endogenous_2022">Battaglini, Patacchini, and Rainone (<a href="#ref-battaglini_endogenous_2022" role="doc-biblioref">2022</a>)</span>, and <span class="citation" data-cites="bramoulle_risk_2007">Bramoullé and Kranton (<a href="#ref-bramoulle_risk_2007" role="doc-biblioref">2007</a>)</span>. Leung estimates preferences from an observed network using estimated equilibrium beliefs. Battaglini, Patacchini, and Rainone impose a joint model of link formation and activity to study an unobserved network. Bramoullé and Kranton study links that permit repeated income sharing. The calculations below state the assumptions behind these different mechanisms and derive their implications.</p>

<h3 data-note-section="1" id="10-447-estimate-equilibrium-beliefs-before-preferences">1. Equilibrium beliefs and two-step estimation</h3>
<p>In Leung’s incomplete-information model, an agent chooses links using observed characteristics, private payoff shocks, and beliefs about other agents’ choices. Equilibrium requires those beliefs to agree with the choice probabilities generated by the model. His symmetry restriction requires observationally equivalent agents to use the same strategy.</p>

<p>A simple threshold model illustrates the two-step argument. Write {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_{ij}\)</span>{% endraw %} for an observed link, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} for a vector of equilibrium link probabilities, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z_{ij}(p)\)</span>{% endraw %} for a payoff index’s regressors, including the relevant beliefs. Suppose</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
D_{ij}=\mathbf1\{z_{ij}(p)&#x27;\theta-\eta_{ij}\ge0\},
\qquad
\eta_{ij}\mid\text{observed characteristics}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The associated probability must satisfy</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-beliefs"></span><div class="math display">\[
p_{ij}=\Phi\{z_{ij}(p)&#x27;\theta\},

\tag{1}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\Phi\)</span>{% endraw %} is the standard normal distribution function. This display describes the probability restriction; it does not assume that shocks across dyads are independent. It is a simplified illustration of the paper’s estimation argument.</p>

<p>The first step estimates the probabilities from the observed network. For example, when characteristics take finitely many values, one can pool links within pairs of types, provided those cells have positive limiting frequencies and a law of large numbers holds for their link averages. The second step inserts the estimated probabilities into the payoff index and estimates {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta\)</span>{% endraw %}. This avoids solving <a href="#eq:nf-beliefs">(1)</a> afresh for every value of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<p>Learning the probabilities and identifying preferences are separate requirements. If two preference vectors generate the same indices on the observed support, estimating {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} accurately will not distinguish them. Consistency also requires the first-step probabilities to describe the equilibrium that generated the data. Symmetry supplies a pooling restriction; it does not by itself supply identification or a law of large numbers.</p>

<p><span id="10-447-why-two-step-estimation-has-a-first-stage-term"></span></p>

<h4 data-note-section="1.1" id="10-447-the-contribution-of-estimated-beliefs">1.1. The contribution of estimated beliefs</h4>
<p>Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M(\theta,p)\)</span>{% endraw %} be a vector of population moments with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M(\theta_0,p_0)=0\)</span>{% endraw %}, and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widehat M\)</span>{% endraw %} be its sample counterpart. For the following calculation, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} are finite-dimensional. Write</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
A=\partial_\theta M(\theta_0,p_0),
\qquad B=\partial_p M(\theta_0,p_0).
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<div id="nf-two-step" class="proposition" data-note-number="1" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 1</strong>. (Two-step limit.) Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A\)</span>{% endraw %} is square and nonsingular, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((\widehat\theta,\widehat p)\xrightarrow{P}(\theta_0,p_0)\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r_n\to\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. Assume</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
r_n\widehat M(\widehat\theta,\widehat p)=o_p(1),
\qquad
r_n\begin{pmatrix}\widehat M(\theta_0,p_0)\\ \widehat p-p_0\end{pmatrix}
\rightsquigarrow
\begin{pmatrix}Z_M\\Z_p\end{pmatrix},
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>and the expansion</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\widehat M(\widehat\theta,\widehat p)
=\widehat M(\theta_0,p_0)
+A(\widehat\theta-\theta_0)+B(\widehat p-p_0)
+o_p\bigl(r_n^{-1}+\|\widehat\theta-\theta_0\|\bigr).
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Then</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-two-step-limit"></span><div class="math display">\[
r_n(\widehat\theta-\theta_0)
\rightsquigarrow-A^{-1}(Z_M+BZ_p).

\tag{2}\]</div>{% endraw %}
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Rearrange the expansion and multiply by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(A^{-1}\)</span>{% endraw %}. The joint convergence makes the fixed-belief moment and the first-step error {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(O_p(r_n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %}. The remaining term is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(o_p(r_n^{-1})+o_p(\|\widehat\theta-\theta_0\|)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Absorbing the last term into the left side gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\widehat\theta-\theta_0=O_p(r_n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %}. Substitution into the expansion makes its remainder {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(o_p(r_n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %}. Multiplication by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r_n\)</span>{% endraw %} and the continuous mapping theorem prove the result. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>

</div>
<p>If the joint limit has finite covariance blocks {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\Omega_{MM},\Omega_{Mp},\Omega_{pM},\Omega_{pp}\)</span>{% endraw %}, the covariance of the limiting preference error is</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
A^{-1}\bigl(\Omega_{MM}+B\Omega_{pp}B&#x27;
+\Omega_{Mp}B&#x27;+B\Omega_{pM}\bigr)A^{-T}.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The two stages can be correlated because they use the same network. A variance calculation that treats estimated beliefs as known omits both their variability and these covariance terms. The proposition assumes the joint limit and the expansion; establishing them for a network model is the substantive asymptotic problem. A function-valued first step requires a corresponding derivative and control of the remainder in an appropriate function space.</p>

<p><span id="10-447-counting-dyads-does-not-count-independent-observations"></span></p>

<h4 data-note-section="1.2" id="10-447-the-number-of-dyads-and-the-estimation-rate">1.2. The number of dyads and the estimation rate</h4>
<p>For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\ge2\)</span>{% endraw %}, consider undirected dyadic observations</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
H_{ij}=U_i+U_j+e_{ij},\qquad i&lt;j,
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>where all node shocks and edge shocks are mutually independent and centered, with variances {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_U^2\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_e^2\)</span>{% endraw %}, respectively. There are {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m=n(n-1)/2\)</span>{% endraw %} dyads. Each node occurs in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n-1\)</span>{% endraw %} of them, so</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\overline H=\frac2n\sum_{i=1}^nU_i+\frac1m\sum_{i&lt;j}e_{ij},
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\overline H)
=\frac{4\sigma_U^2}{n}+\frac{2\sigma_e^2}{n(n-1)}.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>When {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_U^2&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the variance has order {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n^{-1}\)</span>{% endraw %} despite the order-{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n^2\)</span>{% endraw %} dyad count. When {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_U^2=0\)</span>{% endraw %}, it has order {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n^{-2}\)</span>{% endraw %} if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_e^2&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. This example shows why the normalization in Proposition <a href="#nf-two-step">1</a> must be derived from the dependence structure.</p>

<h3 data-note-section="2" id="10-447-an-equilibrium-calculation">2. Joint choices of links and effort</h3>
<p>Battaglini, Patacchini, and Rainone consider agents whose effectiveness depends on effort and on other agents’ effectiveness. For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i=1,\ldots,n\)</span>{% endraw %}, write</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-production"></span><div class="math display">\[
s_i=\sum_{j\ne i}g_{ij}E_j,
\qquad
E_i=\rho s_i^\alpha l_i^{1-\alpha}+\epsilon_i,
\qquad 0&lt;\alpha&lt;1,\quad \rho&gt;0.

\tag{3}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Here {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(l_i\in[0,\overline l]\)</span>{% endraw %} is effort, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E_i\ge0\)</span>{% endraw %} is effectiveness, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(g_{ij}\in[0,\overline g]\)</span>{% endraw %} is the intensity of a directed link. Set {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(g_{ii}=0\)</span>{% endraw %}. The exogenous term {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon_i\)</span>{% endraw %} is nonnegative, and effort costs {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(cl_i\)</span>{% endraw %}, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. A set {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M_i\)</span>{% endraw %} specifies the agents to whom {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i\)</span>{% endraw %} can link; incompatible links are fixed at zero. For a compatible pair, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_{ij}&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} governs the cost</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-cost"></span><div class="math display">\[
C_{ij}(g)
=\frac{g^{1+1/\lambda}}
 {(1+1/\lambda)\theta_{ij}^{(1+\lambda)/\lambda}},
\qquad \lambda&gt;0.

\tag{4}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Their <em>network competitive equilibrium</em> takes other agents’ effectiveness as given in each agent’s optimization. Effort maximizes effectiveness net of effort cost for a given network and effectiveness vector. Links maximize that optimized payoff net of link costs, still taking other agents’ effectiveness as given. Finally, effectiveness must satisfy the production equations at those choices. Thus the behavior used in the link first-order condition holds {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E_j\)</span>{% endraw %} fixed, although equilibrium requires all choices and effectiveness levels to be consistent.</p>

<p>Define</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\kappa=\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)\rho}{c}\right)^{1/\alpha},
\qquad
\delta=\rho\kappa^{1-\alpha}.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<div id="nf-effort" class="proposition" data-note-number="2" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 2</strong>. (Effort and the continuation payoff.) For fixed {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s_i\ge0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the unique optimal effort is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(l_i=\min\{\overline l,\kappa s_i\}\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\kappa s_i\le\overline l\)</span>{% endraw %}, then</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-continuation"></span><div class="math display">\[
E_i=\delta s_i+\epsilon_i,
\qquad
E_i-cl_i=\alpha\delta s_i+\epsilon_i.

\tag{5}\]</div>{% endraw %}
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s_i=0\)</span>{% endraw %}, production is independent of effort, so its positive marginal cost makes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(l_i=0\)</span>{% endraw %} optimal. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s_i&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the objective is strictly concave in positive effort. Its derivative is</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
(1-\alpha)\rho s_i^\alpha l_i^{-\alpha}-c.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>It decreases from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(+\infty\)</span>{% endraw %} to zero at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(l_i=\kappa s_i\)</span>{% endraw %}. Truncating at the effort cap gives the optimum. When the cap does not bind, substitution into production gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E_i=\rho\kappa^{1-\alpha}s_i+\epsilon_i\)</span>{% endraw %}. The definition of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\kappa\)</span>{% endraw %} gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c\kappa=(1-\alpha)\delta\)</span>{% endraw %}, proving the second equality. The formulas also hold at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s_i=0\)</span>{% endraw %} and at equality with the effort cap. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>

</div>
<p><span id="10-447-differentiate-the-link-choice-and-check-the-corners"></span></p>

<p>Suppose the effort cap is inactive for all link vectors under consideration. Proposition <a href="#nf-effort">2</a> makes the link problem separable: agent {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i\)</span>{% endraw %} chooses {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(g_{ij}\)</span>{% endraw %} to maximize {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\delta E_jg_{ij}-C_{ij}(g_{ij})\)</span>{% endraw %} for each {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(j\in M_i\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<div id="nf-links" class="proposition" data-note-number="3" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 3</strong>. (Link choices.) For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, and a marginal benefit {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b\in\mathbb R\)</span>{% endraw %}, the unique maximizer of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(bg-C(g)\)</span>{% endraw %} on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([0,\overline g]\)</span>{% endraw %} is</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
g^*(b)=\begin{cases}
0,&amp;b\le0,\\
\min\{\overline g,\theta^{1+\lambda}b^\lambda\},&amp;b&gt;0.
\end{cases}
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>In the network model, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b=\alpha\delta E_j\ge0\)</span>{% endraw %}, so a compatible link satisfies</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-links"></span><div class="math display">\[
g_{ij}(E_j)=\min\{\overline g,\theta_{ij}^{1+\lambda}(\alpha\delta E_j)^\lambda\}.

\tag{6}\]</div>{% endraw %}
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> The derivative of the cost is</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
C&#x27;(g)=\frac{g^{1/\lambda}}{\theta^{(1+\lambda)/\lambda}}.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>It is continuous and strictly increasing on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([0,\infty)\)</span>{% endraw %}, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(C&#x27;(0)=0\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b\le0\)</span>{% endraw %}, every positive link gives a strictly smaller payoff than zero. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(b&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the objective increases until {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(C&#x27;(g)=b\)</span>{% endraw %} and decreases thereafter. Solving that equality and imposing the cap gives the stated formula. At equality with the cap, the same endpoint is the unique optimum. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>

</div>
<p>Substitution into the effectiveness equation gives a system in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E\)</span>{% endraw %} alone. If all compatible links are below capacity, it becomes</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-interior"></span><div class="math display">\[
E_i=\epsilon_i+\alpha^\lambda\delta^{1+\lambda}
       \sum_{j\in M_i}(\theta_{ij}E_j)^{1+\lambda}.

\tag{7}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The power {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1+\lambda\)</span>{% endraw %} includes both the direct effect of a neighbor’s effectiveness and the response of the chosen link to it. The outcome equation retains the weights {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E_j\)</span>{% endraw %} and the exogenous term {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon_i\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<h3 data-note-section="3" id="10-447-the-resolvent-separates-paths-from-feedback">3. Fixed-network effects and equilibrium feedback</h3>
<p>First, hold {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(G\)</span>{% endraw %} fixed. Write {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r(\delta G)\)</span>{% endraw %} for its spectral radius, to distinguish it from the production parameter {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<div id="nf-resolvent" class="proposition" data-note-number="4" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 4</strong>. (Fixed-network response.) If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r(\delta G)&lt;1\)</span>{% endraw %}, the equation {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E=\delta GE+\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} has the unique solution</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-resolvent"></span><div class="math display">\[
E=R\epsilon,
\qquad
R=(I-\delta G)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\delta^kG^k.

\tag{8}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>For perturbations of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(G\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\delta\)</span>{% endraw %} fixed,</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-differential"></span><div class="math display">\[
dE=R\{\delta(dG)E+d\epsilon\}.

\tag{9}\]</div>{% endraw %}
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> The spectral-radius condition implies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((\delta G)^k\to0\)</span>{% endraw %} and convergence of the matrix geometric series. Multiplying its partial sums by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(I-\delta G\)</span>{% endraw %} gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(I-(\delta G)^{k+1}\)</span>{% endraw %}; taking the limit proves the inverse formula. Differentiating {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((I-\delta G)E=\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((I-\delta G)dE=\delta(dG)E+d\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %}. Multiplication by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(R\)</span>{% endraw %} proves the differential formula. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>

</div>
<p>The {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((i,j)\)</span>{% endraw %} entry of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(G^k\)</span>{% endraw %} sums products of link weights along length-{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k\)</span>{% endraw %} walks from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(j\)</span>{% endraw %}, including walks that revisit vertices. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(G,\delta,\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} are nonnegative, so are {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(R\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E\)</span>{% endraw %}. In particular, the derivative with respect to an externally varied link is</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\frac{\partial E}{\partial g_{ab}}=\delta E_bRe_a,
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(e_a\)</span>{% endraw %} is the {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a\)</span>{% endraw %}th coordinate vector. An increase in the link first changes activity at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a\)</span>{% endraw %} through {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E_b\)</span>{% endraw %}, and that change propagates through the network.</p>

<h4 data-note-section="3.1" id="10-447-a-sufficient-condition-for-a-unique-joint-solution">3.1. A sufficient condition for a unique joint solution</h4>
<p>For endogenous links, define</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
T_i(E)=\epsilon_i+\delta\sum_{j\in M_i}g_{ij}(E_j)E_j,
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>using the capped link rule <a href="#eq:nf-links">(6)</a>. The equilibrium condition is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E=T(E)\)</span>{% endraw %}. The next result gives explicit sufficient conditions for a unique solution in a specified box. It is a direct contraction argument for the displayed model.</p>

<div id="nf-contraction" class="proposition" data-note-number="5" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 5</strong>. (A joint solution on a bounded domain.) Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|M_i|\le m\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\le\epsilon_i\le\overline\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\overline E&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Assume</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-box"></span><div class="math display">\[
\overline\epsilon+\delta m\overline g\,\overline E\le\overline E,
\qquad
\kappa m\overline g\,\overline E\le\overline l,
\qquad
L\triangleq\delta m(1+\lambda)\overline g&lt;1.

\tag{10}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T\)</span>{% endraw %} has exactly one fixed point in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([0,\overline E]^n\)</span>{% endraw %}. Iteration from any vector in that box converges to it. The associated links and efforts form a network competitive equilibrium within the box.</p>

</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(g_{ij}\le\overline g\)</span>{% endraw %}, the first inequality makes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T\)</span>{% endraw %} map the box into itself. The second ensures that the effort formula in Proposition <a href="#nf-effort">2</a> applies to every feasible link vector and effectiveness vector in the box.</p>

<p>For a compatible link, put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a=\theta_{ij}^{1+\lambda}(\alpha\delta)^\lambda\)</span>{% endraw %}. Its contribution before multiplication by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\delta\)</span>{% endraw %} is</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
f(x)=x\min\{\overline g,ax^\lambda\}
     =\min\{\overline g x,ax^{1+\lambda}\},\qquad x\ge0.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Before the cap binds, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f&#x27;(x)=(1+\lambda)ax^\lambda\le(1+\lambda)\overline g\)</span>{% endraw %}. After it binds, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f&#x27;(x)=\overline g\)</span>{% endraw %}. The function is continuous at the joining point, and its right derivative at zero is finite, including when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;\lambda&lt;1\)</span>{% endraw %}. Hence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} is Lipschitz with constant {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((1+\lambda)\overline g\)</span>{% endraw %}. Summing over at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m\)</span>{% endraw %} links gives</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\|T(E)-T(\widetilde E)\|_\infty
\le L\|E-\widetilde E\|_\infty.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The box is complete, so the contraction theorem gives a unique fixed point in the box and convergence of the iteration. The effort and link optimality conditions follow from Propositions <a href="#nf-effort">2</a> and <a href="#nf-links">3</a>; the fixed-point equation gives consistency with production. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>

</div>
<p>The restriction controls the joint feedback, including changes in link intensity. It is sufficient and can be conservative. The proposition makes no uniqueness claim outside the stated domain.</p>

<p>For a two-agent interior example, suppose both activities equal {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, both links equal {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(g(E)=aE^\lambda\)</span>{% endraw %} with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, and both exogenous terms equal {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
E-\delta aE^{1+\lambda}=\epsilon.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>At a solution where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1-(1+\lambda)\delta g(E)\ne0\)</span>{% endraw %}, implicit differentiation along the symmetric branch gives</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\frac{dE}{d\epsilon}
=\frac1{1-(1+\lambda)\delta g(E)}.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Holding the link fixed instead gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1/(1-\delta g)\)</span>{% endraw %}. The additional term comes from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d\{g(E)E\}=(1+\lambda)g(E)dE\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus the fixed-network condition {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\delta g&lt;1\)</span>{% endraw %} need not make the joint iteration locally stable. For this scalar iteration, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((1+\lambda)\delta g&lt;1\)</span>{% endraw %} is sufficient for local stability; equality is a boundary case to which the strict derivative test does not apply.</p>

<h3 data-note-section="4" id="10-447-what-these-models-imply-for-empirical-work">4. What outcomes identify about missing links</h3>
<p>Under the preceding condition that the effort cap is inactive for all feasible link choices, suppose the primitives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha,\delta,\lambda,\theta_{ij}\)</span>{% endraw %} and the link capacities are known. Observing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E\)</span>{% endraw %} then determines the compatible links through <a href="#eq:nf-links">(6)</a>. In empirical work, these primitives must themselves be identified or estimated. The formation equation restricts the network. Solving it conditional on the primitives does not prove that those primitives are identified from the data.</p>

<p>The activity equation alone is much less restrictive. Even if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\delta&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} are known, observing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E\)</span>{% endraw %} identifies only</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\sum_jg_{ij}E_j=\frac{E_i-\epsilon_i}{\delta},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>For example, suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\ge3\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E_2,E_3&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, and both links {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(g_{12},g_{13}\)</span>{% endraw %} are strictly between zero and capacity. For all sufficiently small real {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>{% endraw %}, set</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\widetilde g_{12}=g_{12}+tE_3,
\qquad
\widetilde g_{13}=g_{13}-tE_2,
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>leaving other entries unchanged. These are distinct feasible networks when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\ne0\)</span>{% endraw %}, but</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\widetilde g_{12}E_2+\widetilde g_{13}E_3
=g_{12}E_2+g_{13}E_3.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Consequently, they all satisfy the same activity equation at the observed {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E\)</span>{% endraw %}. If the original network satisfies the strict spectral-radius condition, sufficiently small perturbations also satisfy it by continuity. This is nonidentification under the activity equation alone; the perturbed networks need not satisfy a particular formation model.</p>

<p>Battaglini, Patacchini, and Rainone connect the unobserved network to observed outcomes and characteristics through their parametrized link costs, production model, and distributional assumptions. These restrictions provide information that the unrestricted activity equation lacks. When only some links are recorded, the relation between the true and recorded networks also matters. The <a href="{% post_url 2025-05-08-missing-network-links-and-spillover-bias %}">missing-link exposition</a> derives how an observed exposure can mix several true exposures.</p>

<h3 data-note-section="5" id="10-447-repeated-sharing-is-another-mechanism">5. Repeated sharing on a given network</h3>
<p>Bramoullé and Kranton study links that allow households to share income. After incomes are realized, linked pairs meet repeatedly and divide their current holdings equally. The benefit of a link depends on which risks become pooled in the connected component.</p>

<p>Consider a finite undirected graph on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m\ge2\)</span>{% endraw %} vertices. At each time, draw an edge {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(e=\{i,j\}\)</span>{% endraw %} independently with fixed probabilities {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q_e&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} that sum to one. Replace both selected holdings by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((x_i+x_j)/2\)</span>{% endraw %}, leaving the others unchanged. Write</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
P_e=I-\frac{1}{2}(e_i-e_j)(e_i-e_j)&#x27;,
\qquad x^{(t+1)}=P_ex^{(t)}.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Each update preserves total holdings. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\overline x=m^{-1}\sum_i x_i^{(0)}\)</span>{% endraw %} and define the weighted graph Laplacian</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
L_q=\sum_e q_e(e_i-e_j)(e_i-e_j)&#x27;.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<span id="10-447-an-elementary-convergence-proof-for-repeated-sharing"></span>
<div id="nf-sharing" class="proposition" data-note-number="6" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 6</strong>. (Equal sharing.) If the graph is connected and the initial holdings are finite, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x^{(t)}\to\overline x\mathbf1\)</span>{% endraw %} almost surely. Writing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda_2(L_q)&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} for the smallest positive eigenvalue,</p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:nf-sharing"></span><div class="math display">\[
\mathbb{E}\|x^{(t)}-\overline x\mathbf1\|^2
\le\left(1-\frac{\lambda_2(L_q)}2\right)^t
       \|x^{(0)}-\overline x\mathbf1\|^2.

\tag{11}\]</div>{% endraw %}
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(y=x-\overline x\mathbf1\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(V=\|y\|^2\)</span>{% endraw %}. Averaging vertices {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i,j\)</span>{% endraw %} reduces the sum of their squared deviations by</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
y_i^2+y_j^2-2\left(\frac{y_i+y_j}{2}\right)^2
=\frac12(y_i-y_j)^2.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(V_t\)</span>{% endraw %} is nonincreasing, and independence of the next edge gives</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\mathbb{E}[V_{t+1}\mid x^{(t)}]
=V_t-\frac{1}{2}(y^{(t)})&#x27;L_qy^{(t)}
\le\left(1-\frac{\lambda_2(L_q)}2\right)V_t.
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The last inequality uses {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(y^{(t)}\perp\mathbf1\)</span>{% endraw %}. Connectivity and positive edge weights imply that the kernel of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(L_q\)</span>{% endraw %} consists exactly of constant vectors, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda_2(L_q)&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Also {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{tr}(L_q)=2\)</span>{% endraw %}, hence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda_2(L_q)\le2\)</span>{% endraw %}. Iterating the expectation bound proves <a href="#eq:nf-sharing">(11)</a>. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\le V_t\le V_0\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(V_t\)</span>{% endraw %} decreases, it has an almost-sure limit. Dominated convergence and the expectation bound give zero expectation for that limit, so it is zero almost surely. {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\square\)</span>{% endraw %}</p>

</div>
<p>For a disconnected graph, the same argument applies to each nonsingleton component: the weighted Laplacian has a positive gap on the vectors with zero sum within every component, and each component’s total is preserved. An isolated vertex retains its initial income.</p>

<p><span id="10-447-equal-sharing-needs-both-conservation-and-convergence"></span></p>

<p>For comparison, a deterministic rule {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(x^{(t+1)}=Px^{(t)}\)</span>{% endraw %} needs its own convergence condition. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(P\)</span>{% endraw %} is primitive and doubly stochastic, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(P^t\to\mathbf1\mathbf1&#x27;/m\)</span>{% endraw %}. A connected two-vertex swap matrix is periodic and alternates holdings forever. A primitive row-stochastic matrix need not preserve the total: the matrix with rows {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((0.9,0.1)\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((0.2,0.8)\)</span>{% endraw %} converges to weights {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((2/3,1/3)\)</span>{% endraw %}. These examples explain why the particular averaging rule matters in addition to connectivity.</p>

<h4 data-note-section="5.1" id="10-447-which-risks-are-diversified">5.1. Which risks are diversified</h4>
<p>Suppose a component contains {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m_1\)</span>{% endraw %} households from village 1 and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m_2\)</span>{% endraw %} from village 2, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m=m_1+m_2&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Initial income is</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
x_i^{(0)}=\mu+u_i+v_{a(i)},
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a(i)\)</span>{% endraw %} is the village, the centered household shocks {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u_i\)</span>{% endraw %} have variance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_u^2\)</span>{% endraw %}, the centered village shocks {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v_1,v_2\)</span>{% endraw %} have variance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_v^2\)</span>{% endraw %}, and all these shocks are mutually independent. Equal sharing gives common final income</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\overline x=\mu+\frac1m\sum_i u_i
 +\frac{m_1}{m}v_1+\frac{m_2}{m}v_2,
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>with variance</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[
\operatorname{Var}(\overline x)
=\frac{\sigma_u^2}{m}
 +\sigma_v^2\left[\left(\frac{m_1}{m}\right)^2
                   +\left(\frac{m_2}{m}\right)^2\right].
\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>A larger component diversifies the household-specific risks. The contribution of village shocks depends on the component’s composition: its variance is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_v^2\)</span>{% endraw %} if all households belong to one village and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma_v^2/2\)</span>{% endraw %} when the villages are equally represented. This is the source of the paper’s distinction between adding a link within a community and adding one across communities. An observed link can therefore reflect access to a different risk pool, while an unobserved link can change the sharing arrangement for every household in its component.</p>
