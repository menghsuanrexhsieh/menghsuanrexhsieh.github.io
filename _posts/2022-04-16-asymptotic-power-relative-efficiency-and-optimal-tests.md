<div class="note-content" markdown="0">

<p>Two consistent tests can both have power tending to one at every fixed alternative, while requiring different sample sizes to detect a small departure from the null. Local alternatives retain a nontrivial power comparison as sample size grows. We calculate these power functions, interpret their slopes through relative efficiency, and then use the local likelihood experiment to bound the power of every test.</p>
<p>Throughout, tests take values in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([0,1]\)</span>{% endraw %}, allowing randomization, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z_u=\Phi^{-1}(u)\)</span>{% endraw %} is the lower-tail standard-normal quantile. Thus a level-{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} upper-tail normal test uses {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z_{1-\alpha}\)</span>{% endraw %}. In the iid parametric sections, the score and information come from <a href="{% post_url 2022-04-13-quadratic-mean-differentiability %}">quadratic mean differentiability</a> (QMD), and the likelihood expansion is <a href="{% post_url 2023-03-04-local-asymptotic-normality %}">local asymptotic normality</a> (LAN).</p>
<div id="the-importance-of-testing-sequences-and-asymptotic-relative-efficiency"></div>
<p>First consider simple hypotheses with densities {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_0,p_1\)</span>{% endraw %} relative to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mu\)</span>{% endraw %}. The sum of type-I and type-II error probabilities is</p><span id="eq:test-attains-size"></span>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[S_{0,1}(\varphi)=\mathbb{E}_0\varphi+\mathbb{E}_1(1-\varphi),\qquad S(0,1)=\inf_{0\le\varphi\le1}S_{0,1}(\varphi).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<div id="prop:np-test-attains-inf-size" class="proposition" data-note-number="1" data-note-kind="Proposition"><p><strong>Proposition 1.</strong> A minimizing test equals one where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_1&gt;p_0\)</span>{% endraw %}, zero where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_1&lt;p_0\)</span>{% endraw %}, and can take any value on the equality set.<a href="#fn1" class="footnote-ref" id="fnref1" role="doc-noteref"><sup>1</sup></a> Moreover,</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[S(0,1)=1-\frac12\int|p_1-p_0|\,d\mu=1-\|P_1-P_0\|_{\rm TV}.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p><em>Proof.</em> Write {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(S_{0,1}(\varphi)=1+\int\varphi(p_0-p_1)\,d\mu\)</span>{% endraw %}, and minimize the integrand pointwise. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\int(p_1-p_0)d\mu=0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the integral of its positive part is half its absolute integral. This gives the formula and also describes all minimizers, up to null sets. ◻</p></div>
<p>Finite-sample optimal tests therefore need not be unique. For a fixed-level likelihood-ratio test, the decisions away from its boundary are prescribed, while randomization on the boundary is constrained by the size. Asymptotic optimality allows still more tests to share the same limiting performance.</p>
<h3 data-note-section="1" id="asymptotic-power-functions-and-asymptotic-relative-efficiency">1. Asymptotic Power Functions and Asymptotic Relative Efficiency</h3>
<p>The power is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi_n(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_\theta\varphi_n\)</span>{% endraw %}. For a composite null {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\Theta_0\)</span>{% endraw %}, asymptotic level {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} means {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\limsup_n\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_0}\pi_n(\theta)\le\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}. Pointwise power limits need not exist for arbitrary tests. For two consistent tests, however, the limit is one at each fixed alternative and tells us little about their relative performance near the null.</p>
<div id="eg:sign-test" class="example" data-note-number="2" data-note-kind="Example"><p><strong>Example 2 (Sign test).</strong> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_i\)</span>{% endraw %} have cdf {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F(x-\theta)\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F\)</span>{% endraw %} is continuous at zero, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F(0)=1/2\)</span>{% endraw %}, and zero is its unique median. For testing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta=0\)</span>{% endraw %} against {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, put</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[T_n=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbf1\{X_i&gt;0\},\qquad p(\theta)=1-F(-\theta),\qquad \operatorname{Var}_\theta(T_n)=\frac{p(\theta)(1-p(\theta))}{n}.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The test rejects when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(2\sqrt n(T_n-1/2)&gt;z_{1-\alpha}\)</span>{% endraw %}. Its null rejection probability tends to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_n\to0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the bounded Bernoulli triangular-array CLT gives</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\pi_n(\theta_n)=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha}/2-\sqrt n[p(\theta_n)-1/2]}{\sqrt{p(\theta_n)(1-p(\theta_n))}}\right)+o(1).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The approximation is valid even if the displayed threshold diverges: convergence of cdfs to the continuous normal cdf is uniform in the threshold. Suppose further that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F\)</span>{% endraw %} is differentiable at zero with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f(0)&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. For positive {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_n\to0\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[p(\theta_n)-1/2=f(0)\theta_n+o(\theta_n).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Consequently, if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt n\theta_n\to0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the power tends to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}; if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt n\theta_n\to h\in(0,\infty)\)</span>{% endraw %}, it tends to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1-\Phi(z_{1-\alpha}-2hf(0))\)</span>{% endraw %}; and if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt n\theta_n\to\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}, it tends to one. Thus the nontrivial transition occurs at order {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n^{-1/2}\)</span>{% endraw %}. At each fixed positive alternative, uniqueness of the median gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p(\theta)&gt;1/2\)</span>{% endraw %}, and the law of large numbers proves consistency.</p></div>
<div id="result-3" class="theorem" data-note-number="3" data-note-kind="Theorem"><p><strong>Theorem 3 (Local power).</strong> Suppose, for every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_n\to h\)</span>{% endraw %}, under {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_n=h_n/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %},</p><span id="eq:validity-are"></span>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\frac{\sqrt n[T_n-\mu(\theta_n)]}{\sigma(\theta_n)}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,1).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Assume {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mu\)</span>{% endraw %} is differentiable at zero, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma\)</span>{% endraw %} is continuous there, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma(0)&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Reject when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt n[T_n-\mu(0)]&gt;\sigma(0)z_{1-\alpha}+o(1)\)</span>{% endraw %}, with deterministic cutoff error. Then</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\pi_n(h_n/\sqrt n)\longrightarrow1-\Phi\!\left(z_{1-\alpha}-h\frac{\mu'(0)}{\sigma(0)}\right).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p><em>Proof.</em> Subtract the local mean, use {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt n[\mu(\theta_n)-\mu(0)]\to h\mu'(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}, and apply Slutsky's theorem and normal continuity. This is the sequential formulation of <a href="#ref-van_der_vaart_asymptotic_2007">van der Vaart, Theorem 14.7</a>. Asymptotic level at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} alone does not force this power formula; the calibrated cutoff is part of the statement. ◻</p></div>
<div id="result-4" class="remark" data-note-number="4" data-note-kind="Remark"><p><strong>Remark 4.</strong> The slope or efficacy is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a=\mu'(0)/\sigma(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}. For positive slopes, larger {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a\)</span>{% endraw %} gives higher power at every fixed positive local shift. The relative efficiency of the first test with respect to the second is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((a_1/a_2)^2\)</span>{% endraw %}, with the sample-size convention established in Theorem 7.</p></div>
<div id="result-5" class="remark" data-note-number="5" data-note-kind="Remark"><p><strong>Remark 5 (Third lemma and the two-sample median test).</strong> Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(X_1,\ldots,X_m\)</span>{% endraw %} have cdf {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n\)</span>{% endraw %} have cdf {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F(\cdot-\theta)\)</span>{% endraw %}, independently, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(N=m+n\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m/N\to\lambda\in(0,1)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Assume the location family is QMD, has finite positive information, and has a continuous positive density {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} near its unique median {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}; assume also that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(f\)</span>{% endraw %} is absolutely continuous and tends to zero at negative infinity. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(R_{N,i}\)</span>{% endraw %} be the ascending pooled ranks and put</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[T_N=N^{-1}\sum_{i=m+1}^N\mathbf1\{R_{N,i}\le\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor\}.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Under the null there are no ties. With {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(k=\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor\)</span>{% endraw %}, each rank is uniform, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{E}_0T_N=nk/N^2=n/(2N)+O(N^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %}. The rank projection of <a href="#ref-van_der_vaart_asymptotic_2007">van der Vaart, Theorem 13.5 and Example 14.14</a> gives</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\sqrt N(T_N-n/(2N))=-\frac n{N\sqrt N}\sum_{i=1}^m(\mathbf1\{X_i\le q\}-1/2)+\frac m{N\sqrt N}\sum_{j=1}^n(\mathbf1\{Y_j\le q\}-1/2)+o_{P_0}(1).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The hypotheses match this rank theorem: the underlying null distribution is continuous, the score function is the bounded indicator of the lower half of the ranks, and both sample proportions have nonzero limits. This projection can also be obtained by expanding the pooled empirical median and substituting it into the empirical cdf of the second sample. The null variance is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lambda(1-\lambda)/4\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>For completeness, write {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B_i=\mathbf1\{X_i\le q\}-1/2\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(C_j=\mathbf1\{Y_j\le q\}-1/2\)</span>{% endraw %}. Under the null these are independent, centered, and have variance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1/4\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus the variance of the displayed projection is</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\frac{n^2m+m^2n}{4N^3}=\frac{mn}{4N^2}\longrightarrow\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}4.\]</div>{% endraw %}

<p>The rank theorem uses {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\phi(u)=\mathbf1\{u\le1/2\}\)</span>{% endraw %}, which is continuous except at one point and satisfies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N\phi^2(i/(N+1))\to\int_0^1\phi^2=1/2\)</span>{% endraw %}. Its coefficients are zero in the first sample and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1/N\)</span>{% endraw %} in the second; centering these coefficients gives exactly the two coefficients above. The deterministic centering error {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(nk/N^2-n/(2N)=O(N^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %} disappears after multiplication by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt N\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_N=h/\sqrt N\)</span>{% endraw %}, the local likelihood score is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(-N^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^n f'(Y_j)/f(Y_j)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Its covariance with the projection is</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[-\lambda(1-\lambda)\int_{-\infty}^{q}f'(y)\,dy=-\lambda(1-\lambda)f(q).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The joint CLT and third lemma therefore give local mean {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(-h\lambda(1-\lambda)f(q)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Positive shifts reduce the number of second-sample observations below the pooled median, so the correctly oriented statistic is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(U_N=n/(2N)-T_N\)</span>{% endraw %}, rejecting for large values. Its slope is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(2\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}f(q)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Stating the orientation prevents a sign error from reversing the intended alternative.</p></div>
<div id="result-6" class="proposition" data-note-number="6" data-note-kind="Proposition"><p><strong>Proposition 6 (Consistency).</strong> If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T_n\to\mu(\theta)\)</span>{% endraw %} in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(P_\theta\)</span>{% endraw %}-probability and the rejection cutoffs tend to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mu(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}, then the tests are consistent whenever {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mu(\theta)&gt;\mu(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Alternatively, suppose Theorem 3 holds with positive slope and each power function is nondecreasing. Then, for positive {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_n\)</span>{% endraw %}, power tends to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt n\theta_n\to0\)</span>{% endraw %}, and to one if {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt n\theta_n\to\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. In particular the tests are consistent at every fixed positive alternative.</p>
<p><em>Proof.</em> The first claim is the definition of convergence in probability with a strict gap. For the second, sandwich the power between its values at zero and at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %}, then let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h\downarrow0\)</span>{% endraw %}; for divergent local shifts, bound it below by the power at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %} and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h\to\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. These are the arguments in <a href="#ref-van_der_vaart_asymptotic_2007">van der Vaart, Lemmas 14.15–14.16</a>. ◻</p></div>
<p>Sample-size efficiency needs a precise calibration convention. Fix {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;\alpha&lt;\gamma&lt;1\)</span>{% endraw %}, and for each procedure suppose its size is at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} at every sample size, with asymptotic size {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}. Boundary randomization can be used when needed. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n_i(\theta)\)</span>{% endraw %} be the smallest sample size at which procedure {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(i\)</span>{% endraw %} has power at least {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} against {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<div id="result-7" class="theorem" data-note-number="7" data-note-kind="Theorem"><p><strong>Theorem 7 (Pitman efficiency).</strong> Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\|P_{n,\theta}-P_{n,0}\|_{\rm TV}\to0\)</span>{% endraw %} as {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta\to0\)</span>{% endraw %} for each fixed {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %}. Suppose the two calibrated procedures satisfy Theorem 3 for every convergent sequence of local shifts, with positive slopes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a_i\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then, as {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta\downarrow0\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[n_i(\theta)\sim\frac{(z_{1-\alpha}-z_{1-\gamma})^2}{a_i^2\theta^2},\qquad\frac{n_2(\theta)}{n_1(\theta)}\longrightarrow(a_1/a_2)^2.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>This is a version of <a href="#ref-van_der_vaart_asymptotic_2007">van der Vaart, Theorem 14.19</a>, with the local sequential and finite-sample calibration assumptions made explicit.</p></div>
<div class="proof"><p><em>Proof.</em> Put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_i=(z_{1-\alpha}-z_{1-\gamma})/a_i&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. At sample size {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\lceil(h_i+\epsilon)^2/\theta^2\rceil\)</span>{% endraw %}, local power tends to a number strictly larger than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %}, giving the upper bound on the minimum. For the lower bound, suppose some sample sizes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m\le(h_i-\epsilon)^2/\theta^2\)</span>{% endraw %} reach power {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %}. Bounded {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m\)</span>{% endraw %} are impossible: total-variation continuity and size at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} force their power to stay below {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %}. Along an unbounded subsequence, extract {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt m\theta\to h\in[0,h_i-\epsilon]\)</span>{% endraw %}. The sequential local-power formula gives a limit strictly below {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %}, another contradiction. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon\downarrow0\)</span>{% endraw %}. This proof does not infer monotonicity in sample size from a comparison of two powers. ◻</p></div>
<div id="thm:are-existence" class="theorem" data-note-number="8" data-note-kind="Theorem"><p><strong>Theorem 8 (Random critical values).</strong> In an iid family QMD at an interior point {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_0\)</span>{% endraw %}, suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mu\)</span>{% endraw %} has positive right derivative at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_0\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sigma&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, and for every bounded nonnegative local sequence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_n\)</span>{% endraw %}, under {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_n=\theta_0+h_n/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\sqrt n[T_n-\mu(\theta_n)]\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Reject when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt n[T_n-\mu(\theta_0)]&gt;\hat c_n\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\hat c_n\to\sigma z_{1-\alpha}\)</span>{% endraw %} in null probability. Then the size tends to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}, and for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_n\to h\ge0\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\mathbb{E}_{\theta_n}\varphi_n\longrightarrow1-\Phi\!\left(z_{1-\alpha}-h\frac{\mu'(\theta_0)}\sigma\right).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_j\downarrow\theta_0\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n_j\to\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt{n_j}(\theta_j-\theta_0)=O(1)\)</span>{% endraw %}, and the powers tend to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\beta\in(\alpha,1)\)</span>{% endraw %}, then</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[n_j\sim\frac{(z_{1-\alpha}-z_{1-\beta})^2\sigma^2}{[(\theta_j-\theta_0)\mu'(\theta_0)]^2}.\]</div>{% endraw %}</div>
<div class="proof"><p><em>Proof.</em> LAN implies contiguity along bounded local alternatives, so convergence of the random critical value transfers from the null to these alternatives. Slutsky proves the power formula. For the final assertion, every convergent subsequence of the bounded local shifts must have limit {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((z_{1-\alpha}-z_{1-\beta})\sigma/\mu'(\theta_0)\)</span>{% endraw %}, because the limiting power is strictly increasing in the shift. Thus the entire sequence has this limit. The bounded-shift and diverging-sample-size conditions are needed here; local normality alone says nothing about arbitrary distant alternatives or bounded sample sizes. The minimum-sample-size formulation in Theorem 7 supplies those controls by a different argument. ◻</p></div>
<div id="result-9" class="lemma" data-note-number="9" data-note-kind="Lemma"><p><strong>Lemma 9.</strong> Suppose the family is QMD at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_0\)</span>{% endraw %}, and for each fixed {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h\ge0\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt nT_n\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(hm,\sigma^2)\)</span>{% endraw %} under {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_0+h/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %}, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m,\sigma&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then these limits also hold along {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_n\to h\)</span>{% endraw %}, and the calibrated upper-tail tests have efficacy {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m/\sigma\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p><em>Proof.</em> QMD gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\sqrt{p_{\theta_0+t}}=\sqrt{p_{\theta_0}}+t\dot\ell\sqrt{p_{\theta_0}}/2+o_{L^2}(|t|)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Subtract this expansion at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t=h_n/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t=h/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %}. Their one-observation squared Hellinger distance is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(o(n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %}, so the total-variation distance of the product laws tends to zero. The fixed-shift limit transfers to the varying shift. Subsequences then give centered normality for all bounded shifts, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mu(\theta)=m(\theta-\theta_0)\)</span>{% endraw %}. The cutoff must still be specified as in Theorem 3 or 8; a limit law alone does not define a test. ◻</p></div>
<p>Here is the distance estimate used in Lemma 9. Define {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H^2(P,Q)=\int(\sqrt p-\sqrt q)^2\)</span>{% endraw %} and the affinity {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho(P,Q)=\int\sqrt{pq}=1-H^2(P,Q)/2\)</span>{% endraw %}. Tensorization of the affinity and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(1-(1-x)^n\le nx\)</span>{% endraw %}, for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\le x\le1\)</span>{% endraw %}, give</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[H^2(P^{\otimes n},Q^{\otimes n})=2(1-\rho(P,Q)^n)\le nH^2(P,Q).\]</div>{% endraw %}

<p>Cauchy–Schwarz also gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\|P-Q\|_{\rm TV}\le H(P,Q)\)</span>{% endraw %}, since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\int(\sqrt p+\sqrt q)^2\le4\)</span>{% endraw %}. In Lemma 9 the difference of square-root densities is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((h_n-h)\dot\ell\sqrt{p_{\theta_0}}/(2\sqrt n)+o_{L^2}(n^{-1/2})\)</span>{% endraw %}, so its squared norm is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(o(n^{-1})\)</span>{% endraw %}. The product-law bound therefore tends to zero. It controls the difference of the expectations of every measurable test with values in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([0,1]\)</span>{% endraw %}, including tests whose rejection sets vary with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<h2 data-note-section="2" id="asymptotically-most-powerful-asymptotically-uniformly-most-powerful-and-locally-asymptotically-uniformly-most-powerful-tests">2. Asymptotically Most Powerful, Asymptotically Uniformly Most Powerful, and Locally Asymptotically Uniformly Most Powerful Tests</h2>
<p>Assume now QMD at an interior scalar parameter {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_0\)</span>{% endraw %}, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;I=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0}\dot\ell^2&lt;\infty\)</span>{% endraw %}. Write</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[Z_n=n^{-1/2}\sum_i\dot\ell(X_i),\qquad \log L_{n,h}=hZ_n-\frac12h^2I+o_{P_{\theta_0}}(1).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Under the null {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Z_n\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,I)\)</span>{% endraw %}. The third lemma gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Z_n\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(hI,I)\)</span>{% endraw %} under {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_0+h/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %}, also along {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_n\to h\)</span>{% endraw %} by Lemma 9's Hellinger argument.</p>
<div id="result-10" class="lemma" data-note-number="10" data-note-kind="Lemma"><p><strong>Lemma 10 (Likelihood-ratio power bound).</strong> Fix {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha_n\to\alpha\in(0,1)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\varphi_{n,h}\)</span>{% endraw %} be a Neyman–Pearson test of size exactly {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha_n\)</span>{% endraw %} against {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_0+h/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %}, rejecting above a log-likelihood cutoff {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c_{n,h}\)</span>{% endraw %} and randomizing on equality. Then</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[c_{n,h}\to-\frac12h^2I+h\sqrt I\,z_{1-\alpha},\qquad P_{\theta_0}(\log L_{n,h}=c_{n,h})\to0,\]</div>{% endraw %}
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0+h/\sqrt n}\varphi_{n,h}\to B_\alpha(h):=1-\Phi(z_{1-\alpha}-h\sqrt I).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The same power limit holds for most-powerful tests against {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_0+h_n/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %} when {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_n\to h\ge0\)</span>{% endraw %}. At {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h=0\)</span>{% endraw %}, power tends to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}, but the vanishing-boundary-mass assertion need not hold.</p></div>
<div class="proof"><p><em>Proof.</em> Under the null, the log likelihood has limit {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathcal{N}(-h^2I/2,h^2I)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Its continuous strictly increasing cdf traps the size-calibrated cutoffs at the stated quantile, and makes the boundary mass vanish. Under the alternative, the third lemma changes the mean to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h^2I/2\)</span>{% endraw %}, with the same variance. The upper-tail probability gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B_\alpha(h)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Contiguity also transfers the vanishing probability of the boundary event.</p>
<p>To spell out the cutoff step, let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(W_n=\log L_{n,h}\)</span>{% endraw %}, let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F\)</span>{% endraw %} be its limiting null cdf, and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c=F^{-1}(1-\alpha)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Exact size implies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(P_0(W_n&gt;c_{n,h})\le\alpha_n\le P_0(W_n\ge c_{n,h})\)</span>{% endraw %}. For each {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the limiting tail at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c+\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} is strictly below {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}, and the tail at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c-\epsilon\)</span>{% endraw %} is strictly above it. These inequalities rule out cutoffs outside {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((c-\epsilon,c+\epsilon)\)</span>{% endraw %} eventually. Once the cutoff is inside that interval,</p>

{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[P_0(W_n=c_{n,h})\le P_0(|W_n-c|\le\epsilon),\qquad \limsup_nP_0(W_n=c_{n,h})\le F(c+\epsilon)-F(c-\epsilon).\]</div>{% endraw %}

<p>Letting {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon\downarrow0\)</span>{% endraw %} proves the boundary claim. The same deterministic-cutoff sandwich under the alternative gives the tail at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c\)</span>{% endraw %}; subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation yields {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((c-h^2I/2)/(h\sqrt I)=z_{1-\alpha}-h\sqrt I\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>For varying shifts tending to a positive {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h\)</span>{% endraw %}, LAN and the same argument apply. If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_n\to0\)</span>{% endraw %}, QMD gives total-variation distance tending to zero between the alternative product law and the null product law, so every size-{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha_n\)</span>{% endraw %} test has power tending to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}. This treats the degenerate endpoint without using a nonexistent nondegenerate likelihood quantile. ◻</p></div>
<p>A sequence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\varphi_n\)</span>{% endraw %} is <em>asymptotically most powerful</em> (AMP) at level {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} against a specified alternative sequence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_n\)</span>{% endraw %} if it has asymptotic level at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} and, for every other such sequence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\psi_n\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\limsup_n[\mathbb{E}_{\theta_n}\psi_n-\mathbb{E}_{\theta_n}\varphi_n]\le0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The expectations in this comparison are under the alternatives. For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_n=\theta_0+h/\sqrt n\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, AMP is equivalent to convergence of power to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B_\alpha(h)\)</span>{% endraw %}. To prove the universal upper bound, fix {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\delta&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Eventually {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\psi_n\)</span>{% endraw %} has size at most {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha+\delta\)</span>{% endraw %}, so the Neyman–Pearson lemma and Lemma 10 give {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\limsup_n\mathbb{E}_{\theta_n}\psi_n\le B_{\alpha+\delta}(h)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Send {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\delta\downarrow0\)</span>{% endraw %}. A test attaining the bound is therefore AMP; conversely compare an AMP test with the score test below, which attains the bound. Full power convergence is required: merely having the correct limsup would permit poor power along another subsequence.</p>
<div id="ex:rao-score" class="example" data-note-number="11" data-note-kind="Example"><p><strong>Example 11 (Rao's score test).</strong> Define</p><span id="eq:rao-score"></span>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\overline\varphi_n=\mathbf1\{Z_n&gt;\sqrt I\,z_{1-\alpha}\}.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The null CLT gives asymptotic size {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %}. The third lemma gives, for every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_n\to h\ge0\)</span>{% endraw %},</p><span id="eq:cvg-rao-score-asymp-normal"></span>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0+h_n/\sqrt n}\overline\varphi_n\longrightarrow B_\alpha(h).\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Thus the score test attains the local power bound for every nonnegative finite shift. This is an asymptotic optimality assertion; the normal cutoff does not assert exact finite-sample maximization of the derivative of power.</p>
<p>The convergence is uniform on every compact interval {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\le h\le c\)</span>{% endraw %}. Otherwise a subsequence of shifts would have errors bounded away from zero. Compactness gives a further subsequence converging to some {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h\in[0,c]\)</span>{% endraw %}, including possibly zero. Sequential convergence and continuity of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B_\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} contradict the retained error. Hence</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\sup_{0\le h\le c}|\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0+h/\sqrt n}\overline\varphi_n-B_\alpha(h)|\to0.\]</div>{% endraw %}</div>
<p>Finally, <em>asymptotically uniformly most powerful</em> (AUMP) means that for every asymptotically level-{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>{% endraw %} competitor,</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\limsup_n\sup_{\theta&gt;\theta_0}[\mathbb{E}_\theta\psi_n-\mathbb{E}_\theta\varphi_n]\le0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>Equivalently, this comparison holds along every alternative sequence {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\theta_n&gt;\theta_0\)</span>{% endraw %}, with no restriction on its rate or distance from the null. A fixed local shift does not give an equivalent definition.</p>
<p>The <em>locally asymptotically uniformly most powerful</em> (LAUMP) condition restricts the comparison to bounded local shifts: for every {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %},</p>
{% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\limsup_n\sup_{0&lt;h\le c}[\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0+h/\sqrt n}\psi_n-\mathbb{E}_{\theta_0+h/\sqrt n}\varphi_n]\le0.\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p>The score test is LAUMP under the stated QMD assumptions. If a competitor exceeded it by a fixed amount somewhere in a compact local interval, extract convergent shifts {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h_n\to h\)</span>{% endraw %}. Lemma 10 and the size-{% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha+\delta\)</span>{% endraw %} comparison bound its limiting power by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B_\alpha(h)\)</span>{% endraw %}, whereas the score test converges to that value, a contradiction. At {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h=0\)</span>{% endraw %}, use the total-variation argument from Lemma 10. These local conclusions do not establish global AUMP without additional control away from bounded local alternatives.</p>
