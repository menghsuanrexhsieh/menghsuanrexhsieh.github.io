<div class="note-content" markdown="0">

<p>Throughout, manifolds and maps are smooth unless stated otherwise. On a Riemannian manifold, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\nabla\)</span>{% endraw %} denotes the Levi–Civita connection. The needed local constructions start with <a href="{% post_url 2022-02-05-differentiable-manifolds-charts-constructions-and-examples %}">coordinate charts</a>.</p>

<div id="sphere-theorem" data-note-section="title">

</div>
<p>The sphere theorem asks how tightly positive sectional curvature must be pinched to determine the topology of a manifold. Under the hypotheses below, the curvature bounds force the manifold to be homeomorphic to a sphere. The proof combines an injectivity-radius estimate with comparison geometry to construct two regions that fit together as hemispheres do.</p>

<div id="thm:sphere" class="theorem" data-note-number="1" data-note-kind="Theorem">
<p><strong>Theorem 1</strong> (Sphere Theorem).  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a compact, simply connected differentiable manifold of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\dim M = n\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>{% endraw %} denote its sectional curvature, satisfying </p>

{% raw %}<span id="eq:sphere-theorem-pinching"></span><div class="math display">\[
        0 &lt; h K_{\max} &lt; K \leq K_{\max} ~.\tag{1}\]</div>{% endraw %}
<p> If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h = \frac14\)</span>{% endraw %}, then {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is homeomorphic to a sphere {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb S^n\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

<p>Assume {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is connected and without boundary, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\geq2\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K_{\max}\)</span>{% endraw %} is the global maximum over all tangent two-planes. Rescale the metric by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K_{\max}\)</span>{% endraw %}; then sectional curvatures divide by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K_{\max}\)</span>{% endraw %}. Compactness turns the strict lower bound into</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\frac14&lt;\delta\leq K\leq1\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> for some constant {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\delta\)</span>{% endraw %}. All subsequent lengths refer to this rescaled metric.</p>
</div>
<div id="result-2" class="definition" data-note-number="2" data-note-kind="Definition">
<p><strong>Definition 2</strong>.  The number {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h\)</span>{% endraw %} in <a href="#eq:sphere-theorem-pinching" data-reference-type="eqref" data-reference="eq:sphere-theorem-pinching">(1)</a> is called the <em>pinching number</em>.</p>

<p>More precisely, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h\)</span>{% endraw %} is a lower pinching threshold in the displayed strict inequality; the actual global pinching ratio is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K_{\min}/K_{\max}\)</span>{% endraw %}. A single metric may satisfy the inequality for many smaller values of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(h\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
</div>
<div id="result-3" class="remark" data-note-number="3" data-note-kind="Remark">
<p><strong>Remark 3.</strong> Rauch initiated the pinching approach, and Berger and Klingenberg established the strict quarter-pinched topological sphere theorem. The threshold cannot be lowered in a theorem covering all dimensions: complex projective spaces {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{CP}^m\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m\geq2\)</span>{% endraw %}, have their standard metric with sectional curvatures in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([1,4]\)</span>{% endraw %}, but are not spheres (for instance, their second integral cohomology is nonzero). Thus equality at the quarter-pinching threshold allows additional spaces. See the introduction of <a href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/0705.0766v3">Brendle–Schoen</a> for this historical context and the rank-one symmetric models.</p>
</div>
<p>The conclusion of Theorem 1 concerns the topology of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}. A manifold homeomorphic to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb S^n\)</span>{% endraw %} need not carry its standard differentiable structure: a smooth manifold homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to the standard sphere is called an <em>exotic sphere</em>. Thus a homeomorphism with a sphere does not by itself supply a diffeomorphism. The proof below establishes the homeomorphism asserted in the theorem.</p>
<p>We first obtain the geometric estimates needed for this two-region description. The first result is the estimate of injectivity radius in manifolds of even dimension, which complements Proposition <a href="{% post_url 2023-07-06-the-isotopy-lemma-and-injectivity-radius %}#prop:klingenberg-injectivity-radius-odd" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="prop:klingenberg-injectivity-radius-odd">12</a> (whose proof requires using some elements of Morse theory).</p>

<div id="prop:klingenberg-injectivity-radius-even" class="proposition" data-note-number="4" data-note-kind="Proposition">
<p><strong>Proposition 4</strong> (Klingenberg).  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a simply connected, compact, differentiable manifold of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\dim M = n \geq 2\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %} is even. Furthermore, suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0 &lt; K \leq 1\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\iota (M) \geq \pi\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell=\operatorname{inj}(M)&lt;\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}. Compactness gives a pair {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p,q\)</span>{% endraw %} realizing this nearest cut distance. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(K\leq1\)</span>{% endraw %}, the first conjugate time is at least {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}; hence no minimizing segment of length {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %} from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} is conjugate. The nearest-cut-point lemma gives exactly two such segments. Its global injectivity-radius version joins them smoothly into a closed unit-speed geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma:[0,2\ell]\to M\)</span>{% endraw %}, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p=\gamma(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q=\gamma(\ell)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>A simply connected manifold is orientable, since its orientation double cover is trivial. Parallel transport round {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %} therefore preserves orientation. It fixes {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T=\gamma'\)</span>{% endraw %} and acts on the odd-dimensional normal space by an orientation-preserving orthogonal map. The nonreal eigenvalues occur in conjugate pairs, and the product of all eigenvalues is one; in odd dimension there must be an eigenvalue {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(+1\)</span>{% endraw %}. Its unit eigenvector extends to a nonzero periodic parallel normal field {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(V\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>For the periodic variation {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_s(t)=\exp_{\gamma(t)}(sV(t))\)</span>{% endraw %}, define {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E(s)=\frac12\int_0^{2\ell}|\partial_t\gamma_s|^2dt\)</span>{% endraw %}. There are no endpoint terms, and</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[E'(0)=0,\qquad E''(0)= -\int_0^{2\ell}\langle R(T,V)T,V\rangle\,dt&lt;0.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Thus {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(E(s)&lt;E(0)=\ell\)</span>{% endraw %} for all sufficiently small nonzero {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s\)</span>{% endraw %}. Cauchy–Schwarz gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(L(\gamma_s)^2\leq4\ell E(s)&lt;4\ell^2\)</span>{% endraw %}, so these loops have length less than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(2\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>Choose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t_s\)</span>{% endraw %} maximizing {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(\gamma_s(0),\gamma_s(t))\)</span>{% endraw %}, and put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q_s=\gamma_s(t_s)\)</span>{% endraw %}. The two arcs of the loop give</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d(\gamma_s(0),q_s)\leq\frac12L(\gamma_s)&lt;\ell=\operatorname{inj}(M).\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> At {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s=0\)</span>{% endraw %}, the distance along the original loop is exactly {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\min(t,2\ell-t)\)</span>{% endraw %}: both half-segments minimize and every shorter segment is inside the injectivity radius. Hence its unique maximizing parameter is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}. Uniform convergence of the loops and distances implies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t_s\to\ell\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q_s\to q\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>There is a unique minimizing segment from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q_s\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_s(0)\)</span>{% endraw %}; let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u_s\)</span>{% endraw %} be its initial unit tangent. Its length is less than the injectivity radius, so the distance is smooth near {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q_s\)</span>{% endraw %}. At the maximum along {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_s\)</span>{% endraw %}, first variation gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\langle u_s,\gamma_s'(t_s)\rangle=0\)</span>{% endraw %}. Pass to a convergent subsequence of these unit vectors in a local tangent trivialization. Smooth dependence of geodesics and convergence of their lengths show that the limit {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u\)</span>{% endraw %} starts a minimizing segment from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u\perp T(\ell)\)</span>{% endraw %}. But the only two minimizing segments between these nearest cut points start at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} in the directions {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(T(\ell)\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(-T(\ell)\)</span>{% endraw %}. This is a contradiction. Therefore {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\operatorname{inj}(M)\geq\pi\)</span>{% endraw %}. ◻</p>
</div>
<div id="lem:berger-estimate" class="lemma" data-note-number="5" data-note-kind="Lemma">
<p><strong>Lemma 5</strong> (Berger).  Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} be a compact differentiable manifold, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p, q \in M\)</span>{% endraw %} such that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,q) = \operatorname{diam}(M)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Then, for all {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w \in T_p M\)</span>{% endraw %}, there exists a minimizing geodesic {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma(0) = p\)</span>{% endraw %} connects to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, such that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\langle \gamma&#x27;(0), w \rangle \geq 0\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>

</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> This is the critical-point property of a maximum of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d_q\)</span>{% endraw %}, including possible cut points. Here is a direct first-variation argument. If the conclusion failed for a nonzero {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w\)</span>{% endraw %}, compactness of the set of minimizing initial directions from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} would give a uniform negative bound on {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\langle u,w\rangle\)</span>{% endraw %}. Put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_t=\exp_p(tw)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Every sequence of minimizing segments from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_t\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}, as {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\downarrow0\)</span>{% endraw %}, has a subsequence converging to a minimizing segment from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus their unit initial directions {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u_t\)</span>{% endraw %}, compared in local coordinates, satisfy {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\langle u_t,w_t\rangle\leq-c&lt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} for small {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>{% endraw %}, where {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w_t\)</span>{% endraw %} is the velocity of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_t\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>Choose a fixed small {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, and let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(z_t\)</span>{% endraw %} be the point at distance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\eta\)</span>{% endraw %} along such a segment from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p_t\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %}. Uniform normal neighborhoods give the Taylor estimate</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d(p,z_t)=\eta+t\langle u_t,w_t\rangle+O(t^2),\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> with an error uniform over these directions. Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p_t,q)=\eta+d(z_t,q)\)</span>{% endraw %}, the triangle inequality implies</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d(p_t,q)-d(p,q)\geq\eta-d(p,z_t)\geq ct-O(t^2)&gt;0.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> This contradicts the diameter-maximizing property of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p,q\)</span>{% endraw %}. For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(w=0\)</span>{% endraw %}, any minimizing segment works. ◻</p>
</div>
<div id="lem:brown-berger" class="lemma" data-note-number="6" data-note-kind="Lemma">
<p><strong>Lemma 6 (Berger–Tsukamoto covering).</strong> Suppose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is compact and simply connected, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\frac14&lt;\delta\leq K\leq1\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,q)=D=\operatorname{diam}M\)</span>{% endraw %}. For every</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt\delta}&lt;r&lt;\pi,\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> one has {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M=B_r(p)\cup B_r(q)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Both balls are normal coordinate balls, by the even- and odd-dimensional injectivity estimates. The lower endpoint of this interval must be strict: on the unit round sphere, two open balls of radius {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi/2\)</span>{% endraw %} centered at antipodes omit the equator.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> The interval is nonempty because {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\delta&gt;1/4\)</span>{% endraw %}. Suppose a point {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u\)</span>{% endraw %} lies outside both balls. Put {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a=d(p,u)\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c=d(q,u)\)</span>{% endraw %}, so {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\leq a,c\leq D\)</span>{% endraw %}. Choose a minimizing segment from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(u\)</span>{% endraw %}, and use Lemma 5 to choose a minimizing segment from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} making angle {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\alpha\leq\pi/2\)</span>{% endraw %} with it. Bonnet–Myers gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D\leq\pi/\sqrt\delta\)</span>{% endraw %}. Toponogov hinge comparison and the spherical cosine law yield</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\cos(\sqrt\delta\,c)\geq \cos(\sqrt\delta\,a)\cos(\sqrt\delta\,D) +\sin(\sqrt\delta\,a)\sin(\sqrt\delta\,D)\cos\alpha&gt;0.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Both cosine factors in the product are strictly negative, because {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(a,D\geq r&gt;\pi/(2\sqrt\delta)\)</span>{% endraw %}, while the sine term is nonnegative. This remains valid at an antipodal model endpoint by continuity. But {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(c\geq r&gt;\pi/(2\sqrt\delta)\)</span>{% endraw %} implies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\cos(\sqrt\delta c)&lt;0\)</span>{% endraw %}, a contradiction. The injectivity estimates give {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r&lt;\operatorname{inj}(M)\)</span>{% endraw %}, so the two balls are diffeomorphic to Euclidean balls. ◻</p>
</div>
<div id="result-7" class="remark" data-note-number="7" data-note-kind="Remark">
<p><strong>Remark 7.</strong> The covering proof uses the global hinge comparison. Intersections of metric spheres do not themselves provide totally geodesic normal neighborhoods, so the archival localization claim cannot justify replacing this step by a local Rauch comparison. The diameter bound used here is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\pi/\sqrt\delta\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
</div>
<div id="lem:equidistant-divisions-klingenberg-berger" class="lemma" data-note-number="8" data-note-kind="Lemma">
<p><strong>Lemma 8.</strong> Fix {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\)</span>{% endraw %} in the interval of Lemma 6. For each unit {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v\in T_pM\)</span>{% endraw %}, the segment {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v(t)=\exp_p(tv)\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0\leq t\leq r\)</span>{% endraw %}, contains exactly one point {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m(v)=\gamma_v(\rho(v))\)</span>{% endraw %} with</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d(p,m(v))=d(q,m(v)),\qquad 0&lt;\rho(v)&lt;r.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> The functions {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho\)</span>{% endraw %} and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(m\)</span>{% endraw %} are continuous in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v\)</span>{% endraw %}. The analogous assertion holds with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p,q\)</span>{% endraw %} exchanged.</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof.</em> Since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r&lt;\operatorname{inj}(M)\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(p,\gamma_v(t))=t\)</span>{% endraw %}. The function</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\varphi_v(t)=d(q,\gamma_v(t))-t\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> is continuous and nonincreasing: for {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(s&lt;t\)</span>{% endraw %}, the triangle inequality gives {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(d(q,\gamma_v(t))\leq d(q,\gamma_v(s))+t-s\)</span>{% endraw %}. It is positive at zero and negative at {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\)</span>{% endraw %}, since {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v(r)\notin B_r(p)\)</span>{% endraw %} and the covering lemma puts it in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B_r(q)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Thus a zero exists in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((0,r)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>If there were two zeros {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(0&lt;s&lt;t&lt;r\)</span>{% endraw %}, then</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[d(q,\gamma_v(t))=t=s+(t-s)=d(q,\gamma_v(s))+d(\gamma_v(s),\gamma_v(t)).\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> A minimizing segment from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v(s)\)</span>{% endraw %}, followed by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v|_{[s,t]}\)</span>{% endraw %}, would consequently be minimizing. A minimizing broken geodesic has no corner, so it agrees by uniqueness of the geodesic equation with the segment from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\gamma_v(t)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Its initial point at distance {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(t\)</span>{% endraw %} from the terminal point must then be {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %}, giving {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(q=p\)</span>{% endraw %}, a contradiction. Therefore there is one zero, with strict opposite signs on its two sides.</p>
<p>If {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v_j\to v\)</span>{% endraw %}, every convergent subsequence of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho(v_j)\in[0,r]\)</span>{% endraw %} has limit a zero of {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\varphi_v\)</span>{% endraw %}, by continuity of the exponential map and distance. Uniqueness forces that limit to be {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho(v)\)</span>{% endraw %}; hence the full sequence converges. This proves continuity, including uniform separation of the root from zero and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\)</span>{% endraw %} by compactness of the unit sphere. ◻</p>
</div>
<div class="proof">
<p><em>Proof of Theorem 1.</em> Normalize the curvature as above and choose {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\in(\pi/(2\sqrt\delta),\pi)\)</span>{% endraw %}. Let {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p,q\)</span>{% endraw %} realize the diameter. The injectivity estimate is Proposition 4 in even dimensions and the preceding note's odd-dimensional Klingenberg estimate in odd dimensions. Define</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[D_p=\{x:d(p,x)\leq d(q,x)\},\qquad D_q=\{x:d(q,x)\leq d(p,x)\},\qquad \Sigma=\{x:d(p,x)=d(q,x)\}.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> These are closed, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_p\cup D_q=M\)</span>{% endraw %}, and {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_p\cap D_q=\Sigma\)</span>{% endraw %}. The covering lemma implies {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_p\subset B_r(p)\)</span>{% endraw %}: if a point lies instead in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(B_r(q)\)</span>{% endraw %}, its distance to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(p\)</span>{% endraw %} is no larger and hence is still less than {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\)</span>{% endraw %}. Likewise {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_q\subset B_r(q)\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>For {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(v\in S_pM\)</span>{% endraw %}, Lemma 8 shows that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\varphi_v\)</span>{% endraw %} is positive before {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho(v)\)</span>{% endraw %} and negative after it. Thus</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[D_p=\{\exp_p(tv):v\in S_pM,\ 0\leq t\leq\rho(v)\},\qquad \Sigma=\{\exp_p(\rho(v)v):v\in S_pM\}.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> In particular, the continuous radial map</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[F_p:\overline B_1(0)\subset T_pM\longrightarrow D_p,\qquad F_p(sv)=\exp_p(s\rho(v)v),\quad F_p(0)=p,\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> is bijective. Continuity at zero follows from the uniform bound {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho(v)&lt;r\)</span>{% endraw %}. Compactness of the domain and the Hausdorff property make it a homeomorphism. Its unit-sphere image is exactly {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\Sigma=\partial D_p\)</span>{% endraw %}, since the strict sign change occurs on every radial segment. The same construction gives a homeomorphism {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F_q\)</span>{% endraw %} from a closed unit ball onto {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(D_q\)</span>{% endraw %}, with {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\partial D_q=\Sigma\)</span>{% endraw %}.</p>
<p>To make the gluing explicit, the two boundary parameterizations determine a homeomorphism</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[h=(F_q|_{S_qM})^{-1}\circ(F_p|_{S_pM}):S_pM\longrightarrow S_qM.\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Extend it radially by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H(sv)=s\,h(v)\)</span>{% endraw %}, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(H(0)=0\)</span>{% endraw %}. This is a homeomorphism of closed balls; the continuity of the inverse follows from the same radial formula. View {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb S^n\)</span>{% endraw %} as two closed {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(n\)</span>{% endraw %}-balls with equal boundary points identified. Map the northern ball by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F_p\)</span>{% endraw %} and the southern ball by {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(F_q\circ H\)</span>{% endraw %}. The two maps agree on the common boundary, so they define a continuous map onto {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}. They are injective on the separate interiors, whose images are disjoint by the strict distance inequalities, and on the common boundary. Hence the map is a continuous bijection from the compact sphere onto the Hausdorff manifold {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %}, and is a homeomorphism.</p>
<p>The two hemisphere images meet <em>on their common boundary</em>; they are not disjoint when that boundary is included. Also, the equidistant distance is {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\rho(v)&lt;r\)</span>{% endraw %}, generally depending on direction, rather than the constant {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(r\)</span>{% endraw %}. ◻</p>
</div>
<div id="result-9" class="remark" data-note-number="9" data-note-kind="Remark">
<p><strong>Remark 9.</strong> The preceding radial gluing proves a homeomorphism; it does not supply smoothness across the interface or at the two centers. The stronger conclusion that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is diffeomorphic to a sphere follows from the differentiable sphere theorem. <a href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/0705.0766v3">Brendle–Schoen, Theorem 1</a>, proves the more general result that a compact manifold of dimension at least four with strictly pointwise quarter-pinched curvature is diffeomorphic to a spherical space form; simple connectivity makes that space form the sphere. The low-dimensional cases use the corresponding surface and three-dimensional results.</p>
<p><a href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/0807.1900v1">Petersen–Tao, Theorem 1.1</a>, gives a dimension-dependent {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\varepsilon(n)&gt;0\)</span>{% endraw %} for which a closed simply connected manifold with sectional curvatures in {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\([1/4-\varepsilon(n),1]\)</span>{% endraw %} is diffeomorphic to a sphere or a compact rank-one symmetric space. These are cited extensions; their Ricci-flow proofs are not part of the elementary proof above.</p>
</div>
<div id="result-10" class="remark" data-note-number="10" data-note-kind="Remark">
<p><strong>Remark 10.</strong> <a href="https://doi.org/10.4310/jdg/1214436922">Hamilton's 1982 theorem, Theorem 1.1</a>, says that a closed three-manifold with positive Ricci curvature admits a metric of constant positive sectional curvature. Thus a connected such manifold is diffeomorphic to a spherical space form {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb S^3/\Gamma\)</span>{% endraw %}, and is a sphere if it is simply connected. Without that hypothesis, {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{RP}^3\)</span>{% endraw %} and lens spaces are counterexamples to the sphere-only conclusion.</p>
<p>In dimension four, <a href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0309287v1">Chang–Gursky–Yang, Theorem A and its following remarks</a>, assumes a smooth closed manifold with positive Yamabe constant {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y(M,[g])\)</span>{% endraw %} of its conformal class and</p>
<p> {% raw %}<div class="math display">\[\int_M|W_g|^2\,dv_g&lt;16\pi^2\chi(M).\]</div>{% endraw %}</p>
<p> Here {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(W_g\)</span>{% endraw %} is the <em>Weyl tensor</em>, distinct from {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(Y(M,[g])\)</span>{% endraw %}, and the norm is the full {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\((0,4)\)</span>{% endraw %}-tensor norm {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(|W|^2=\sum_{i,j,k,l}W_{ijkl}^2\)</span>{% endraw %} in an orthonormal frame. The conclusion is that {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(M\)</span>{% endraw %} is diffeomorphic to {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb S^4\)</span>{% endraw %} or {% raw %}<span class="math inline">\(\mathbb{RP}^4\)</span>{% endraw %}. The norm convention matters: viewing Weyl curvature as an operator on two-forms changes its squared norm by a factor of four. These external theorems have different hypotheses from sectional quarter-pinching.</p>
</div>
