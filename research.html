Publications

  1. How Taxes Affect Growth: Evidence from Cross-Country Panel Data with K. Kawano, J. Olson, J. Slemrod · 2026. International Tax and Public Finance, 33(2), 375–444. doi
    Synopsis

    We ask how much the estimated growth effect of tax policy depends on the methodological choices a researcher makes. Using cross-country panel data, we estimate the effects of personal, corporate, and value-added tax rate changes under alternative measures of policy shocks, using both estimators from the earlier literature and newer ones that account for staggered treatment. The estimates are imprecise, and we find no credible support for the claim that tax rate changes have a statistically robust medium-term effect on national income.

  2. Tangential Wasserstein Projections with F. Gunsilius & M. Lee · 2024. Journal of Machine Learning Research, 25(69), 1–41. doi
    Synopsis

    We develop a notion of projection between sets of probability measures from the geometry of the 2-Wasserstein space, working on its regular tangent cones by way of generalized geodesics. The construction handles general multivariate measures, is computationally efficient, and gives a unique solution in regular settings. Applied to causal inference, it generalizes synthetic controls to multivariate data with individual-level heterogeneity, and estimates optimal weights jointly over all time periods.

  3. Revisiting the Analysis of Matched-Pair and Stratified Experiments in the Presence of Attrition with Y. Bai, J. Liu & M. Tabord-Meehan · 2024. Journal of Applied Econometrics, 39(2), 256–268. doi
    Synopsis

    Applied advice on matched-pair experiments with attrition is contradictory: dropping pairs containing an attrited unit is variously described as helpful or harmful, and stratifying into larger groups is often proposed as the fix. We derive what the difference-in-means estimator actually targets in a matched-pair design, both when observations from attrited pairs are retained and when they are dropped. We find limited support for the claim that dropping pairs recovers the average treatment effect, though it may recover a convex weighted average of conditional average treatment effects; similar findings hold for stratified designs with and without strata fixed effects.

  2. Have the Growth Effects of Tax Policy Changed Over Time? with W. Gale, L. Kawano, A. Saini, S. Thorpe, S. Wyse · 2026. working paper
    Synopsis

    We extend Romer and Romer’s narrative analysis of exogenous tax shocks through 2019, identifying an exogenous tax increase in 2013 and a tax cut in 2018. Over the full 1950–2019 period, tax cuts have the same substantial growth effects they found through 2007 — but the average conceals a break: tax cuts substantially boosted growth in 1950–1984 and had minimal effect on GDP in 1985–2019, after the income tax was indexed for inflation and the Great Moderation began. The changing response of the federal funds rate to tax changes is qualitatively consistent with that shift, though estimates of how much of it monetary policy explains span a wide range.

  3. Pricing AI Services with Data-Use Rights with F. Zhou, Z. Hu · 2026. Submitted. working paper
    Synopsis

    An AI platform sells access today while deciding which customer interactions may train tomorrow’s model, a tension absent from standard service menus: the usage price that protects a restricted-use premium tier from cannibalization may be too high to procure valuable training usage. We study nonlinear menus in which each contract specifies an access fee, a usage price, and a data-use right, and we characterize when targeted data procurement beats blanket train-all or restrict-all policies. The relevant value of data turns out to be menu-adjusted — continuation-value gains net of user burdens, information rents, and any repair needed to preserve self-selection — which yields a select–price–repair–retire rule for designing these menus.

  4. Stacked Triple Differences 2026. Submitted. working paper
    Synopsis

    Under staggered adoption, the conventional three-way fixed-effects implementation of triple differences inherits the interpretation problems now well understood in the difference-in-differences literature. I extend the stacked DiD approach to the DDD setting by building self-contained stacks — treated and clean comparison cohorts, each with treatment-eligible and ineligible units — and prove that a saturated regression on the appended data identifies a strictly positive, cell-size-weighted average of stack-level conditional average treatment effects. The method trades efficiency for regression-based transparency, pairwise rather than global parallel changes-in-trends, and direct control over both comparison groups and aggregation weights.

  5. External Validity in Empirical Public Finance: With an Application to Taxable Income Elasticities with G. Sanz-Maldonado, J. Slemrod · 2026. Revision at Journal of Public Economics. working paper
    Synopsis

    Estimating the elasticity of taxable income from one country’s data implicitly assumes nothing can be learned from other countries’ experience — an assumption that is wrong, but that needs a principled framework to relax. We propose a three-step empirical Bayes framework, grounded in statistical decision theory, that models cross-country heterogeneity as a function of the structural determinants of the tax system, so the data decide endogenously how much each country’s estimate should be informed by external evidence, leaning harder on it where within-country estimates are imprecise. Applying it to 27 OECD countries, we characterize how ETI estimates should be revised and how to predict behavioral responses for countries that have not adopted a given tax.

  6. The Rise of Within-Region Inequality: Analysis Using a Shift-Share-Like Instrument 2024. working paper
    Synopsis

    I ask whether the rise of knowledge-intensive economic activity widened disparities within U.S. cities over three decades. Existing shift-share instruments built from patenting activity and citations require fixing the set of patent shocks a priori, which sits badly with the fact that patenting arises randomly and differentially across regions; I instead construct a shift-share-like instrument from regional shocks and inter-region diffusion links, allowing the diffusion network to be endogenous and identifying off shocks that are as good as randomly assigned. Local expansion in knowledge-intensive activity produces positive and nontrivial increases in income and education segregation, while its effect on occupational segregation is statistically insignificant.
