  1. How Taxes Affect Growth: Evidence from Cross-Country Panel Data with K. Kawano, J. Olson, J. Slemrod · 2026. International Tax and Public Finance, 33(2), 375–444. doi

    We test whether the measured effect of tax changes on growth survives different reasonable choices of method. Looking at personal, corporate, and value-added tax changes with both older estimators and newer ones built for staggered timing, the estimates are too imprecise to show a medium-term effect on national income.

    We evaluate the implications of different methodological choices for estimating the effect of tax policy on economic growth using cross-country panel data. We estimate the effect of personal, corporate, and value-added tax rate changes using alternative measures of policy shocks and employing estimation strategies both from the previous literature, and from newer methods that account for staggered treatments. Overall, we find imprecise estimates that do not credibly support claims that tax rate changes have a statistically robust medium-term impact on national income.

  2. Tangential Wasserstein Projections with F. Gunsilius & M. Lee · 2024. Journal of Machine Learning Research, 25(69), 1–41. doi

    We use the geometry of optimal transport to project one set of probability distributions onto another, tractably and with a unique answer in regular cases. Applied to causal inference, it extends synthetic control to multivariate, individual-level data, with weights chosen jointly across all time periods.

    We develop a notion of projections between sets of probability measures using the geometric properties of the 2-Wasserstein space. It is designed for general multivariate probability measures, is computationally efficient to implement, and provides a unique solution in regular settings. The idea is to work on regular tangent cones of the Wasserstein space using generalized geodesics. Its structure and computational properties make the method applicable in a variety of settings, from causal inference to the analysis of object data. An application to estimating causal effects yields a generalization of the notion of synthetic controls to multivariate data with individual-level heterogeneity, as well as a way to estimate optimal weights jointly over all time periods.

  3. Revisiting the Analysis of Matched-Pair and Stratified Experiments in the Presence of Attrition with Y. Bai, J. Liu & M. Tabord-Meehan · 2024. Journal of Applied Econometrics, 39(2), 256–268. doi

    Advice on what to do when a unit drops out of a matched pair contradicts itself. We work out what the usual difference-in-means comparison estimates in that case, and find little support for the common recommendation to discard the affected pairs, though discarding them can recover a convex weighted average of conditional effects.

    In this paper we revisit some common recommendations regarding the analysis of matched-pair and stratified experimental designs in the presence of attrition. Our main objective is to clarify a number of well-known claims about the practice of dropping pairs with an attrited unit when analyzing matched-pair designs. Contradictory advice appears in the literature about whether or not dropping pairs is beneficial or harmful, and stratifying into larger groups has been recommended as a resolution to the issue. To address these claims, we derive the estimands obtained from the difference-in-means estimator in a matched-pair design both when the observations from pairs with an attrited unit are retained and when they are dropped. We find limited evidence to support the claims that dropping pairs helps recover the average treatment effect, but we find that it may potentially help in recovering a convex weighted average of conditional average treatment effects. We report similar findings for stratified designs when studying the estimands obtained from a regression of outcomes on treatment with and without strata fixed effects.

  2. Have the Growth Effects of Tax Policy Changed Over Time? with W. Gale, L. Kawano, A. Saini, S. Thorpe, S. Wyse · 2026. working paper

    We bring Romer and Romer's narrative measure of exogenous tax changes up through 2019. Tax cuts look as expansionary as ever over the full postwar period, but that average hides a break: they raised GDP substantially before 1985 and barely moved it afterward. The federal funds rate responded differently to tax changes on either side of that break.

    We extend Romer and Romer's (RR, 2010) classic narrative analysis of exogenous tax shocks to cover data through 2019 and identify an exogenous tax increase in 2013 and an exogenous tax cut in 2018. We find that tax cuts have the same substantial effects on growth over the 1950-2019 period as RR found for 1950-2007. Several major events circa 1985 – the indexing of the income tax for inflation, the onset of the Great Moderation, and changes in monetary policy – could plausibly have changed the effects of tax shocks. We test this idea and find that although tax cuts substantially boosted growth in 1950–1984, they had minimal effects on GDP in 1985-2019. Changes in monetary policy before and after 1985 – specifically, in the federal funds rate's response to tax changes – are qualitatively consistent with the changing GDP response to taxes. Rough estimates of the share of the GDP response due to monetary policy span a wide range.

  3. External Validity in Empirical Public Finance: With an Application to Taxable Income Elasticities with G. Sanz-Maldonado, J. Slemrod · 2026. Revision at Journal of Public Economics. working paper

    Estimating how much taxable income responds to tax rates one country at a time throws away what other countries have learned. We give a procedure that borrows across countries according to how similar their tax systems are and how noisy each country's own estimate is, and apply it to 27 OECD countries.

    Estimates of the elasticity of taxable income (ETI) based on data from only one country implicitly assume nothing can be learned from the experience of other countries. This assumption is wrong, but rigorously relaxing it requires a principled framework to aggregate information from different contexts. We propose a general three-step empirical Bayes framework grounded in statistical decision theory that accomplishes this by modeling cross-country heterogeneity as a function of the structural determinants of the tax system. The framework determines endogenously how much each country's estimate should be informed by external evidence, placing more weight on cross-country evidence for countries whose within-country estimates are imprecise. We apply our framework to 27 OECD countries, characterize how ETI estimates should be revised taking into account evidence from other countries, and outline how to predict behavioral responses for countries that have not adopted a given tax.

  4. Pricing AI Services with Data-Use Rights with F. Zhou, Z. Hu · 2026. Submitted. working paper

    An AI platform prices access today while deciding whose conversations it may learn from tomorrow. The price that keeps premium customers from downgrading can be too high to attract useful training data. We show when it pays to buy data from one tier rather than training on everyone or no one, and which data-sharing discounts to retire once their value falls below the rents they cost.

    AI service platforms sell current access while deciding which customer interactions may be used for future model improvement. This creates a tension absent from standard service menus: the usage price that protects premium restricted-use plans from cannibalization may be too high to procure valuable training usage, while the price that buys data may undermine premium separation. We study nonlinear service menus in which each contract specifies an access fee, a usage price, and a data-use right. Against blanket data policies that either train on all served usage or restrict all served usage, we characterize when targeted data procurement is optimal. A low-tier data-sharing plan can buy useful interactions and, when premium users have higher training-use burdens, make the cheap plan less attractive to them. The relevant value of data is menu-adjusted: continuation-value gains net of user burdens, information rents, and any repair needed to preserve self-selection. Numerical analyses illustrate that targeted procurement can outperform train-all policies even while collecting less raw data. The analysis yields a select--price--repair--retire rule: select tiers whose data value survives these costs, price data sharing as procurement rather than a generic subsidy, repair menus that create positive self-selection cycles, and retire discounts once marginal continuation value falls below the rent burden.

  5. Stacked Triple Differences 2026. Submitted. working paper

    I extend the stacked difference-in-differences design to triple differences, comparing each treated group with a clean comparison group inside self-contained blocks. Unlike the standard three-way fixed-effects regression when treatment starts at different times, this provably averages the underlying effects with positive weights.

    Triple differences (DDD) is a workhorse quasi-experimental design in applied economics. But, under staggered adoption, its conventional three-way fixed-effects (3WFE) implementation inherits the interpretation issues now well understood in the difference-in-differences literature. I introduce stacked DDD. I extend the stacked difference-in-differences approach to the DDD setting by creating self-contained stacks, each consisting of four cells over an event window: treated and clean comparison cohorts, each with treatment-eligible and treatment-ineligible units. Appending these stacks yields a unified dataset for estimating treatment effects. I prove that, at each post-treatment event-time, a linear regression with fully saturated fixed-effects applied to the stacked dataset identifies a strictly positive, cell-size-weighted average of stack-level conditional average treatment effects, with stack weights proportional to stack-level cell sizes. Building on this characterization, I outline alternative weighting schemes that recover causal estimands with clear interpretations. Stacked DDD complements recent GMM and imputation-based frameworks by trading efficiency for regression-based transparency, pairwise (rather than global) parallel changes-in-trends, and direct control over both the comparison group for each treated unit and the aggregation weights. I provide two empirical illustrations where stacked DDD yields substantially different quantitative conclusions compared to existing procedures.

  6. The Rise of Within-Region Inequality: Analysis Using a Shift-Share-Like Instrument 2024. working paper

    I ask whether the growth of knowledge-intensive work pulled American cities apart internally. Existing shift-share instruments require deciding in advance which patent shocks count; I build one that lets shocks land randomly across regions and travel along a diffusion network that is itself endogenous. Income and education segregation rise by sizable amounts, while the effect on occupational segregation is statistically insignificant.

    I study whether the rise in knowledge-intensive economic activities led to increased economic disparities in U.S. cities over three decades. While existing works have sought to combine patenting activities and patenting citations to construct shiftshare instruments for knowledge-intensive economic activities, I argue this strategy does not apply cleanly, because it requires fixing the set of patent shocks a priori, whereas patenting activities arise randomly and differentially across regions. I propose a shift-share-like instrumental variable framework that leverages regional-level shocks and inter-region diffusion links as inputs in its construction. I allow shocks to arise randomly and differentially across regions, and I allow the observed diffusion network to be endogenous. Identification is achieved through assuming shocks are "as good as randomly assigned". I show that the asymptotic framework does not require observing a large set of regions, but rather large numbers of realized shocks across regions. Using the proposed instrumental variable framework, I find local expansion in knowledgeintensive activities leads to positive and nontrivial increases in income and education segregation in U.S. cities, but its effect on occupation segregation is statistically insignificant.
